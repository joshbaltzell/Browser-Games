# 100 Remarkable Things Single Cells Do

Compiled 2026-11-16 as the factual basis for skill cards in a game about microbes.

## Introduction

This catalogue lists 100 distinct abilities of single-celled organisms (bacteria, archaea and single-celled eukaryotes), including behaviours of colonies, biofilms and aggregates of single cells. Each entry names at least one real organism and gives the mechanism, figures, cost, trigger, limits, a confidence rating and the sources consulted.

How to read it:

- **Numbers** are given only where a source consulted for this catalogue supports them. Where sources disagree, the range or the disagreement is stated. "No reliable figure found" means none was verified, not that none exists.
- **Energy and cost** is mostly qualitative. Few of these abilities have a measured energy budget. Where a figure exists (for example protons per flagellar revolution), it is given.
- **Confidence**: high = textbook and well replicated; medium = well supported but details debated; low = recent, single-lab or contested.
- **Sources** are pages consulted during research (search result pages and abstracts; most full texts were not read end to end). Primary papers and reviews were preferred; a few entries lean on university pages or Wikipedia where nothing better surfaced, and this is noted.
- Overstated popular claims (slime-mould "intelligence", Stentor "learning", bacterial "memory") are reported as what was actually measured.

Category counts: Defence 8, Attack and competition 8, Reproduction 8, Mating and gene exchange 8, Resource management 8, Movement 9, Sensing 9, Chemical production 8, Dormancy and extremes 9, Cooperation 8, Construction 9, Anything else 8. Total 100.

## 1. Defence

### 1. Viral mugshot memory (CRISPR)
- **Category:** Defence
- **Organism:** *Streptococcus thermophilus* (bacterium; the yoghurt and cheese starter strain DGCC7710)
- **What it does:** When a virus attacks and the cell survives, it keeps a scrap of the virus's DNA filed in its own genome. If that virus ever comes back, the cell recognises the sequence and cuts the invader apart. The filed scraps are inherited, so the cell's children are born immune.
- **How it works:** New "spacers" of phage DNA are inserted into a CRISPR array between repeat sequences. The array is transcribed and processed into short guide RNAs that load into a Cas nuclease (Cas9 in this species), which scans incoming DNA for a matching protospacer next to a short PAM motif and cuts it. Resistance requires the spacer to match the phage sequence exactly.
- **Numbers:** Adding a single new spacer in CRISPR1 was the most frequent outcome of a phage challenge; adding more spacers raised resistance further. No reliable figure found for the per-cell energy cost.
- **Energy and cost:** Cheap to store (tens of base pairs per memory) but carries real risks: a spacer that matches the cell's own DNA is lethal, and CRISPR immunity blocks beneficial gene uptake too. Expressing Cas proteins is a standing protein cost.
- **What triggers it:** Surviving a phage infection (acquisition); detecting a matching sequence (interference).
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Point mutations in the phage protospacer or PAM escape the memory; many phages carry anti-CRISPR proteins. Immunity is sequence-specific, so a novel phage is not covered.
- **Confidence:** High. The 2007 *Science* result has been replicated worldwide and underpins CRISPR biotechnology.
- **Sources:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17379808/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/2928866

### 2. Infected-cell self-destruct (CBASS)
- **Category:** Defence
- **Organism:** *Vibrio cholerae* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** When a virus gets inside, the cell makes a chemical alarm signal that tells it to destroy its own membrane. The cell dies, but so does the virus factory inside it, so the infection never spreads to its neighbours.
- **How it works:** A CD-NTase enzyme (DncV in *Vibrio*) synthesises the cyclic dinucleotide 3'3'-cGAMP on infection. cGAMP allosterically activates the phospholipase CapV, which degrades membrane lipids and kills the cell before the phage finishes replicating — "abortive infection". The system is an evolutionary relative of animal cGAS–STING immunity.
- **Numbers:** A survey found CBASS systems widespread in bacteria, with 2,199 systems (39% of those surveyed) carrying additional accessory genes. Expressing the *V. cholerae* CBASS in *E. coli* confers broad phage resistance.
- **Energy and cost:** The ultimate cost: the individual cell's whole future. The standing cost is expression of two or more proteins; the catastrophic cost is paid only on triggering.
- **What triggers it:** Phage infection. In *V. cholerae* the system is also under quorum-sensing and folate-depletion control.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Only protects the clone, not the individual. False triggering is fatal. Some phages encode cyclic-nucleotide-degrading anti-CBASS proteins.
- **Confidence:** High for the mechanism (Cohen et al. 2019, *Nature* 574:691–695); medium for the ecological importance of each variant.
- **Sources:** https://www.weizmann.ac.il/molgen/Sorek/files/publications/Cohen_Nature_2019.pdf ; https://www.nature.com/articles/s41564-023-01556-y

### 3. Persister sleep
- **Category:** Defence
- **Organism:** *Escherichia coli* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** A small fraction of cells in any growing population spontaneously stop growing for a while. Antibiotics that work by attacking growth cannot touch them, so when the drug is washed away these sleepers wake and rebuild the population.
- **How it works:** Single-cell microscopy in microfluidic devices showed cells switching stochastically between a normally growing state and a slow- or non-growing persister state. Switching rates were measured as independent of whether antibiotic was present, so this is pre-existing phenotypic heterogeneity, not an induced response. Persistence is not genetic resistance: regrown persisters are as drug-sensitive as the parent.
- **Numbers:** Classic estimates put wild-type *E. coli* persisters at roughly 1 in 10^5–10^6 cells (Moyed and Bertrand 1983, cited in later work); *hip* high-persistence mutants, used in the single-cell work, make far more.
- **Energy and cost:** The cost is forgone growth: persisters do not reproduce while the population around them does. In a drug-free world they are pure loss, which is why the fraction stays small — classic bet-hedging.
- **What triggers it:** Largely spontaneous switching; stress and stationary phase raise the fraction.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** No protection against agents that kill non-growing cells; prolonged therapy that outlasts the sleep eventually kills them.
- **Confidence:** Medium-high. The phenomenon is solid (Balaban et al. 2004, *Science* 305:1622–1625); which molecular mechanisms dominate (toxin–antitoxin modules, ATP depletion, stochastic ppGpp) is still argued, and several early toxin–antitoxin papers were later retracted or disputed.
- **Sources:** https://perso.crans.org/frenoy/compbiol/articles/Balaban2004.pdf ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1449587

### 4. Antibiotic shredder (beta-lactamase)
- **Category:** Defence
- **Organism:** *Escherichia coli* and *Klebsiella pneumoniae* carrying TEM-1 (bacteria)
- **What it does:** The cell secretes an enzyme into the space just outside its inner membrane that chops up penicillin-type antibiotics before they can reach their target. One cell's enzyme can also protect nearby cells by clearing the drug from the shared neighbourhood.
- **How it works:** TEM-1 is a class A serine beta-lactamase. It opens the four-membered beta-lactam ring by forming a covalent acyl-enzyme with an active-site serine, then hydrolyses it. Arg244 holds the substrate's carboxyl group and stabilises the transition state. The target it protects is the penicillin-binding proteins that build the cell wall.
- **Numbers:** TEM-1 is described in the literature as having high catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) for penicillins and early cephalosporins, but poor activity on oxyimino-cephalosporins such as ceftazidime. I did not verify a specific kcat value, so: no reliable figure found for turnover number.
- **Energy and cost:** Protein synthesis plus, usually, the burden of carrying a plasmid. Plasmid carriage typically costs a few percent of growth rate in the absence of drug, which is why resistance can decline when antibiotic use stops.
- **What triggers it:** Often constitutive from a plasmid; some beta-lactamases are induced by cell-wall damage fragments.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Specific to one drug class; beaten by inhibitor combinations (clavulanate) and by drugs TEM-1 cannot open. Single point mutations are needed to extend its range, and those mutations usually cost stability.
- **Confidence:** High for the mechanism and clinical importance; medium for any particular kinetic constant quoted elsewhere.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4400348 ; https://espace.inrs.ca/14609/1/Site-saturation%20mutagenesis%20of%20Tyr-105%20reveals%20its%20importance%20in%20substrate%20stabilization%20and%20discrimination%20in%20TEM-1%20beta-lactamase.pdf

### 5. Coat swap (antigenic variation)
- **Category:** Defence
- **Organism:** *Trypanosoma brucei* (flagellate; African sleeping sickness)
- **What it does:** The parasite wears a dense coat of a single protein. Just as the host's immune system learns to recognise that coat and starts killing, a few parasites have already switched to a completely different one and the infection carries on. The cycle repeats for months.
- **How it works:** The cell expresses one variant surface glycoprotein (VSG) at a time from one of roughly 15 telomeric expression sites. Switching happens either transcriptionally (silencing the active site and activating another) or by recombination, copying a silent VSG gene into the active site. The coat is about 15 nm thick and covers the whole cell, shielding invariant surface proteins from antibodies.
- **Numbers:** The genome carries a large VSG family: estimates range from over 1,000 VSG-encoding genes to about 2,000 genes and pseudogenes depending on whether pseudogenes are counted. About 15 telomeric expression sites. I did not verify the often-quoted figure of ~10^7 VSG molecules per cell, so treat that number as unconfirmed.
- **Energy and cost:** An enormous share of the genome is dedicated to an archive that is mostly never used, plus the continuous synthesis of a dense surface coat. Recombination-based switching risks genome damage.
- **What triggers it:** Switching is largely stochastic and ongoing; the host antibody response selects the switched minority rather than causing the switch.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Does not prevent any individual parasite from being killed — it saves the population, not the cell. Eventually the archive and the host both run down.
- **Confidence:** High for the mechanism; medium for repertoire size and switching rate, which vary by strain and method.
- **Sources:** https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6162063/ ; https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/191940/1/1_s2.0_S1369527422000935_main.pdf

### 6. Trichocyst burst
- **Category:** Defence
- **Organism:** *Paramecium tetraurelia*, *P. caudatum*, *P. jenningsi* (ciliates)
- **What it does:** Thousands of tiny spear-shaped packages sit just under the cell's skin. When a predator grabs the cell, the packages fire outward and expand into long needles in an instant, jamming the attacker and pushing the *Paramecium* away.
- **How it works:** Trichocysts are dense-core secretory vesicles docked at the plasma membrane. A stimulus causes membrane fusion, and contact with extracellular Ca2+ triggers an explosive, cooperative recrystallisation of the matrix proteins that elongates the spindle-shaped contents several-fold. No ATP is consumed in the expansion itself — it is a stored-conformation spring released by calcium.
- **Numbers:** Trichocyst-non-discharging mutants were eaten 9 to 45 times faster than wild-type cells by the predatory ciliate *Dileptus*, with similar encounter rates for both, showing the defence acts at the capture stage. Membrane fusion after stimulation occurs within about 30 ms. Trichocyst release is described as the fastest dense-core vesicle exocytosis known.
- **Energy and cost:** Building and loading thousands of vesicles is a standing protein and membrane cost; fired trichocysts must be replaced. The discharge itself is powered by stored chemical conformation plus external calcium, not by ATP at the moment of firing.
- **What triggers it:** Predator contact; also triggered experimentally by lysozyme, picric acid and other agents.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Works against *Dileptus* and *Monodinium balbiani* but is not effective against *Didinium nasutum*. Spent trichocysts leave the cell temporarily defenceless.
- **Confidence:** High (Harumoto and Miyake 1991 and later work; Plattner's reviews).
- **Sources:** https://www.nies.go.jp/chiiki1/protoz/refere/id7999/7328.htm ; https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/jeu.12332

### 7. Grow wings on warning
- **Category:** Defence
- **Organism:** *Euplotes octocarinatus* (ciliate)
- **What it does:** When a predator is nearby, this normally egg-shaped cell smells a chemical the predator sheds and grows broad lateral "wings" over a day or two. The wider shape makes it much harder for the predator to swallow.
- **How it works:** The predator *Lembadion bullinum* sheds a surface protein kairomone into the water; a "Lembadion factor" was isolated and shown to induce the change from the ovoid form into a larger laterally winged morph. A different predator, *Amoeba proteus*, sheds a peptide kairomone of roughly 5,000–10,000 Da that instead raises escape behaviour and cell size.
- **Numbers:** Rejection by a predator shifts from roughly 1:1 for ovoid morphs to about 2:1–20:1 for winged morphs, depending on predator and prey species. With *Amoeba proteus* present, avoidance behaviour rose from 16 ± 5% to 84 ± 5% after 14 h, and cell width from about 59 to 77 µm within 48 h.
- **Energy and cost:** Wings cost material and increase drag; the defence is inducible precisely because it is not worth paying for when no predator is present.
- **What triggers it:** Dissolved predator kairomone.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Takes many hours — useless against a sudden attack. Defence is predator-specific: morphologically defended cells did not show behavioural defences against all predators.
- **Confidence:** High for induction; medium for the exact protection factors, which vary by species pair.
- **Sources:** https://www.zobodat.at/pdf/Verh-Ges-Oekologie_28_1997_0493-0498.pdf ; https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/BF00006691.pdf

### 8. Pressure-release valve (MscL)
- **Category:** Defence
- **Organism:** *Escherichia coli* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** If a cell in salty water is suddenly hit by fresh water, water rushes in and the cell is about to burst. A huge pore in its membrane snaps open and dumps the contents' solutes out in milliseconds, dropping the pressure and saving the cell.
- **How it works:** MscL is a pentameric channel gated directly by tension in the lipid bilayer, not by a ligand. Tension tilts the transmembrane helices and opens a very wide, poorly selective pore that vents ions and small osmolytes. It still works when purified into artificial membranes, proving bilayer tension is the gate.
- **Numbers:** Open-pore estimates: 2.5–3 nm diameter from structural work; ~9–10 nm² cross-section inferred from the ~3 nS conductance (about 3.4 nm diameter). MscL has four conducting states plus a closed state, and a high tension threshold near the lytic limit.
- **Energy and cost:** Essentially free to operate — no ATP, the energy is in the osmotic gradient. The cost is losing accumulated solutes that were expensive to make or import, so the cell must rebuild its osmolyte pool afterwards.
- **What triggers it:** Osmotic downshock producing near-lytic membrane tension.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Indiscriminate — it loses valuable metabolites with the water. If it opens inappropriately the cell is poisoned by its own leakiness; this is why its threshold sits so high.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Sources:** https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10279863/ ; https://rupress.org/jgp/article-pdf/113/4/525/1192269/gp-7779.pdf

## 2. Attack and competition

### 9. Spring-loaded spear (T6SS)
- **Category:** Attack and competition
- **Organism:** *Vibrio cholerae* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** The cell builds a long spring inside itself, loaded like a crossbow. When it touches a rival cell, the spring collapses and drives a poisoned spike straight through the rival's membranes, injecting toxins. Then the cell recycles the spring and reloads.
- **How it works:** The T6SS is a structural relative of a contractile phage tail. An outer sheath assembles around an inner Hcp tube tipped with a VgrG/PAAR spike carrying effector toxins. Sheath contraction ejects the tube through the cell's own outer membrane and into the neighbour. ClpV unfolds the contracted sheath for reuse. Immunity proteins protect sister cells carrying the same system.
- **Numbers:** Assembly in 20–30 s; contraction to about half length in under 5 ms (2012), with a later study reporting under 2 ms; disassembly over 30–60 s. The *V. cholerae* competence-linked T6SS gene set comprises about 13 components.
- **Energy and cost:** Each firing consumes a whole multi-protein sheath and tube, plus ATP for ClpV-driven recycling. Firing at an immune or non-target cell wastes the shot entirely.
- **What triggers it:** Growth on chitin surfaces and the competence regulators TfoX and QstR; in other species, contact or being attacked ("tit-for-tat").
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Strictly contact-dependent, so it is useless in open water. Cells carrying the matching immunity proteins are unharmed.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Sources:** https://authors.library.caltech.edu/29830 ; https://www.nature.com/articles/nrmicro2775

### 10. Invade and eat from inside
- **Category:** Attack and competition
- **Organism:** *Bdellovibrio bacteriovorus* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** This tiny fast-swimming predator rams another bacterium, bores through its wall, and settles inside the space between the victim's membranes. It digests the victim from within, grows into a long filament, chops itself into several new predators and bursts out.
- **How it works:** A single sheathed polar flagellum drives high-speed search swimming. On attachment it degrades the prey peptidoglycan locally and enters the periplasm, sealing the prey into a rounded "bdelloplast". Prey macromolecules are imported and used directly; the predator elongates, then septates into progeny that regrow flagella and lyse the husk.
- **Numbers:** Swimming speeds reported from about 35–60 up to 160 µm/s. Whole predatory cycle about 2–3 h (one textbook says 4 h); progeny are released roughly 4 h after invasion in one study.
- **Energy and cost:** Attack-phase cells cannot grow and burn reserves while hunting; a failed attack is a dead end. Living inside the prey gives access to ready-made amino acids and nucleotides, which is unusually cheap biosynthesis.
- **What triggers it:** Encounter with susceptible Gram-negative prey; prey presence raises swimming speed.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Cannot attack Gram-positive cells; decoy species and biofilm structure reduce success. High-speed swimming is energetically costly and attack-phase cells starve if prey is scarce.
- **Confidence:** High for the life cycle; medium for exact speeds and cycle times, which vary with prey and temperature.
- **Sources:** https://bionumbers.hms.harvard.edu/bionumber.aspx?id=110476 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3133215

### 11. Wolf-pack hunting
- **Category:** Attack and competition
- **Organism:** *Myxococcus xanthus* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** These cells hunt in coordinated swarms. They glide across a surface together, surround a colony of other bacteria, and kill it with a combination of secreted enzymes, antibiotics and direct contact. Then the whole group feeds on the released nutrients.
- **How it works:** Two motility systems (adventurous single-cell gliding and social type IV pilus motility) are both needed to prey efficiently. Killing uses two modes: a short-range mode based on collective secretion of secondary metabolites and hydrolytic enzymes that lyse prey and degrade their macromolecules, and contact-dependent killing relying on Tad-like and type III secretion-like systems. Metals also feature, both as oxidative stressors imposed on prey and as a resource contested with prey.
- **Numbers:** No reliable figure found for a per-cell kill rate verified here.
- **Energy and cost:** Secreted enzymes and antibiotics are public goods — expensive to make and freely available to any neighbour, including cheats. Hunting only pays when enough cells share the cost, which is why the behaviour is collective.
- **What triggers it:** Contact with prey cells and prey-derived cues; starvation drives the group towards both predation and fruiting-body development.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Requires a surface and a dense swarm; dilute conditions break the economics. Prey with resistance or their own secretion systems can win.
- **Confidence:** High that predation is collective and multi-modal; medium on the relative weight of each killing mechanism, which is actively debated.
- **Sources:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2024.1339696/full ; https://academic.oup.com/femsre/article-pdf/33/5/942/18141882/33-5-942.pdf

### 12. Suicide-bomb toxin (colicins)
- **Category:** Attack and competition
- **Organism:** *Escherichia coli* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** A small fraction of a colony kills itself in order to release a protein poison that punches holes in, or shreds the nucleic acids of, closely related competitor strains. The dying cells' relatives carry an antidote gene and are unaffected.
- **How it works:** Colicin genes sit on plasmids beside an immunity gene and a lysis gene, and are induced by the SOS DNA-damage response. Colicin is released only when the lysis protein kills the producing cell. Colicin E1 binds a surface receptor, translocates, and forms a voltage-dependent ion channel in the inner membrane that collapses the proton motive force; other colicins are nucleases.
- **Numbers:** Survival follows single-hit kinetics at low numbers of adsorbed colicin E1 molecules, deviating around 50 molecules per cell, and the channel is a single-molecule structure — so very few molecules per target cell are lethal. "One molecule kills a cell" is an idealisation of this result rather than a measured certainty.
- **Energy and cost:** The producer dies. Immunity protein must be made constantly by every relative. The strategy pays only because the killed competitors are not relatives.
- **What triggers it:** SOS response (DNA damage, stress, some antibiotics); a stochastic subpopulation induces spontaneously.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Only kills cells bearing the right surface receptor; receptor-loss mutants are resistant, which drives rock-paper-scissors dynamics among producer, sensitive and resistant strains.
- **Confidence:** High for the mechanism; medium for the strict "single molecule" phrasing, which is an extrapolation from single-hit kinetics.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC323714 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colicin

### 13. Harpoon and tow line
- **Category:** Attack and competition
- **Organism:** *Polykrikos kofoidii* (dinoflagellate)
- **What it does:** This single cell carries a two-part weapon: a sticky probe and a coiled harpoon. The probe touches the prey first, the harpoon fires and punches through the prey's armour, and then the attacker reels the prey in on a tether.
- **How it works:** High-speed filming of the taeniocyst–nematocyst complex showed a sequence: the taeniocyst adheres to the prey, the nematocyst discharges and punctures it, and the prey is retrieved using a tow filament. A stylet caps the coiled tube, punctures the capsule, pops off the lid and penetrates armoured prey. Unlike cnidarian nematocysts, the dinoflagellate version does not use the same chemical propellant.
- **Numbers:** No reliable figure found for discharge speed or force in this organism.
- **Energy and cost:** Building the complex is a major subcellular construction project; each capsule is single-use. Firing at the wrong target wastes a whole weapon.
- **What triggers it:** Physical contact with prey; isolated taeniocysts also discharge spontaneously, so the trigger threshold is low.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Single-use; the taeniocyst's exact role is still unclear, and the suggestion that it releases an adhesive is a hypothesis.
- **Confidence:** High that it fires and captures prey (Gavelis et al. 2017, *Science Advances*); low-to-medium on the taeniocyst's function.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5375639 ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/artful-amoeba/marine-microbes-exchange-fire-with-elaborate-subcellular-weapons-video/

### 14. Toxic-dart capture
- **Category:** Attack and competition
- **Organism:** *Didinium nasutum* (ciliate) hunting *Paramecium*
- **What it does:** A barrel-shaped hunter rams its snout into a *Paramecium* several times its own bulk, fires anchoring darts and poison darts into it, hauls it in by its own internal currents, squeezes the water out of it and swallows it whole.
- **How it works:** Contact with a hungry *Didinium*'s proboscis fires two extrusome types: short pexicysts, which attach to the prey surface without penetrating, and long toxicysts, which penetrate while their proximal ends stay embedded in the proboscis. A rise in cytoplasmic Ca2+ is the actual cause of toxicyst discharge. Cytoplasmic streaming pulls the toxicysts inward, dragging the attached prey into a temporary cytostome; the prey is compressed and its volume reduced by fluid elimination before the cytostome closes and the proboscis reforms.
- **Numbers:** No reliable figure verified here for prey-to-predator size ratio or handling time.
- **Energy and cost:** Extrusomes are consumed per attack and must be rebuilt; ingestion deforms the whole cell. A single large meal, however, can fund a division.
- **What triggers it:** Contact with prey when hungry — satiated cells do not fire.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Notably, *Paramecium* trichocysts do not protect against *Didinium*; but the hunter is itself slow and vulnerable, and its specialised diet means it starves without *Paramecium*.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Sources:** https://www.nies.go.jp/chiiki1/protoz/refere/id1999/1170.htm ; https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF01279249

### 15. Origami neck
- **Category:** Attack and competition
- **Organism:** *Lacrymaria olor* (ciliate)
- **What it does:** A teardrop-shaped cell shoots out a thin "neck" tipped with a feeding head, sweeping the water far beyond its own body to find prey, then pulls it all back in. It can do this over and over for its whole life.
- **How it works:** The membrane and cortex store the extra surface area in curved-crease folds — about 15 helical pleats wrapping the cell — which unfurl when the neck extends and refold when it retracts. The geometry is a curved-crease origami with topological singularities, letting the surface deploy and store repeatedly without tearing.
- **Numbers:** A cell roughly 40–60 µm long extends its neck up to about 1,200 µm (reported as up to 1,500 µm in some coverage) in about 30 s — on the order of 30 times its body length. Secondary accounts give repeat counts from over 20,000 to 50,000 cycles in a lifetime; the 1,200 µm abstract figure is the most authoritative number here.
- **Energy and cost:** The fold geometry means surface area is stored, not synthesised, each cycle — a key reason the motion is repeatable. Driving extension and retraction costs cytoskeletal work; no verified ATP figure.
- **What triggers it:** Hunting behaviour; neck sweeps appear to be exploratory rather than purely reactive.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** The extended neck is fragile and exposes the cell; the mechanism is a recent finding and the energetics are not yet quantified.
- **Confidence:** High for the observation and the fold mechanism (Flaum and Prakash, *Science* 384, eadk5511, 2024); medium for the lifetime cycle counts, which differ across sources.
- **Sources:** https://physicsworld.com/a/shapeshifting-organism-uses-cellular-origami-to-extend-to-30-times-its-body-length/ ; https://www.sciencenews.org/article/protist-neck-origami-unfold-lacrymaria

### 16. Nibble to death (trogocytosis)
- **Category:** Attack and competition
- **Organism:** *Entamoeba histolytica* (amoeba; amoebic dysentery)
- **What it does:** Instead of swallowing a human cell whole, the amoeba takes repeated bites out of it. After enough bites the human cell's membrane fails and it dies. The same biting lets the parasite tunnel into the gut lining.
- **How it works:** The amoeba attaches via surface lectins and internalises fragments of the target cell's membrane and cytoplasm — "amoebic trogocytosis". Ingesting the bites is required for the killing: blocking PI3K signalling or microfilament dynamics reduced both trogocytosis and target-cell death. The process also operates during invasion of intestinal tissue explants.
- **Numbers:** No reliable figure verified here for bites per kill or time to death.
- **Energy and cost:** Requires actin remodelling and membrane traffic at the biting site, and sustained contact with a live target — slower than secreting a toxin but it works on cells that resist whole-cell phagocytosis.
- **What triggers it:** Contact with live host cells; dead cells are instead engulfed whole.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Contact-dependent and slow; blocked by inhibitors of actin dynamics. The finding overturned the earlier model that secreted effectors kill cells before ingestion, so some older literature conflicts.
- **Confidence:** High (Ralston et al. 2014, *Nature* 508:526–530); the broader claim that trogocytosis is ancient and widespread is medium.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4006097 ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/amoeba-takes-bites-of-human-cells-to-kill-them/

## 3. Reproduction and division

### 17. Treadmilling division ring
- **Category:** Reproduction and division
- **Organism:** *Bacillus subtilis*, *Escherichia coli* (bacteria)
- **What it does:** To split in two, the cell builds a ring of protein filaments around its waist. The filaments do not pull the ring tight like a drawstring; instead they march around the circumference, dragging the wall-building machinery with them so new wall is laid down in a circle until the cell is pinched apart.
- **How it works:** FtsZ, a tubulin relative, polymerises into short filaments anchored by FtsA. The filaments treadmill (adding subunits at one end, losing them at the other), so individual molecules stay put while the filament moves. Peptidoglycan synthases follow the moving filaments; in *B. subtilis* the FtsZ treadmilling rate controls the rate of septal peptidoglycan synthesis and of division.
- **Numbers:** Single molecules of FtsZ and FtsA are immobile within moving filaments, confirming treadmilling. FtsAZ motion was unaffected by several peptidoglycan-synthesis inhibitors. No reliable nm/s treadmilling velocity verified here.
- **Energy and cost:** GTP hydrolysis by FtsZ drives treadmilling; cell-wall synthesis costs sugar precursors and the proton motive force used for their transport. Division is the single most expensive coordinated act in a cell cycle.
- **What triggers it:** Completion of chromosome replication and positional signals (Min system, nucleoid occlusion) that permit ring assembly at midcell.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Misplaced rings produce DNA-less minicells. FtsZ is a prime antibiotic target precisely because the ring is essential and has no backup.
- **Confidence:** High (Bisson-Filho et al. 2017, *Science* 355:739–743, and the companion *E. coli* study).
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5485650 ; https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/02/170216143852.htm

### 18. Pole-to-pole oscillator finds the middle
- **Category:** Reproduction and division
- **Organism:** *Escherichia coli* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** A cell with no ruler still divides exactly in half. It does it with a protein that sloshes back and forth from one end to the other every minute or so. Because the protein spends least time in the middle, and because it blocks the division ring wherever it is, the ring can only form at the centre.
- **How it works:** MinD binds the membrane with ATP and recruits the FtsZ inhibitor MinC. MinE forms a ring that strips MinD off the membrane by stimulating its ATPase, chasing it to the opposite pole; the cycle repeats. The time-averaged MinC concentration is lowest at midcell, so FtsZ can only assemble there.
- **Numbers:** Reported oscillation periods differ: about 50 s in the Meinhardt–de Boer model, 40–50 s for GFP-Min in vivo, and roughly 1–2 min in Howard et al.'s analysis. MinD dwells in each half for about 10 s in MinE-positive cells. FtsZ-associated early division proteins counter-oscillate at the same period.
- **Energy and cost:** Continuous ATP hydrolysis by MinD, spent purely on positional information — the cell burns energy to compute where its middle is.
- **What triggers it:** Runs continuously throughout the cell cycle.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Fails in cells of abnormal length (multiple nodes form); *min* mutants make minicells. It is a geometry-dependent mechanism, so it does not transfer to very differently shaped bacteria, which use other systems.
- **Confidence:** High for the mechanism; medium for a single canonical period, which depends on strain and conditions.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC64659 ; https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2015.00478/full

### 19. One cell, two destinies
- **Category:** Reproduction and division
- **Organism:** *Caulobacter crescentus* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** Every time this bacterium divides it makes two different cells: one stays anchored to a surface by a stalk and gets on with copying its DNA, while the other is born with a flagellum, swims off to find somewhere new, and cannot copy its DNA until it settles and grows a stalk of its own.
- **How it works:** The master regulator CtrA is phosphorylated and active in the swarmer compartment, where it represses replication initiation; it is cleared from the stalked compartment, allowing a new replication round. Asymmetry is set up by an imbalance in phosphorylated DivK between compartments, and SpmX localisation to the future stalked pole triggers the swarmer-to-stalked transition, which coincides with replication initiation.
- **Numbers:** In a 180-minute cell cycle, FtsZ forms a ring at the division plane at about 90 minutes, with visible constriction roughly 30 minutes later.
- **Energy and cost:** The swarmer phase is a reproductive holiday — the cell invests in a flagellum and chemotaxis machinery it will later discard, and delays replication to buy dispersal.
- **What triggers it:** The intrinsic cell cycle; surface contact and nutrient conditions modulate the swarmer period.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Half of all offspring do not reproduce immediately, so growth rate is sacrificed for dispersal. The regulatory network is brittle: CtrA mistiming is lethal.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3587792 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2192755

### 20. Live birth of internal offspring
- **Category:** Reproduction and division
- **Organism:** *Epulopiscium* sp. type B (bacterium; surgeonfish gut symbiont)
- **What it does:** This giant bacterium never splits in two. Instead, several complete daughter cells grow inside the mother, fill her up, and then emerge when she breaks open — on a daily schedule tied to the fish's feeding.
- **How it works:** Type B cells reproduce solely by forming multiple internal offspring, a mode thought to have evolved from endospore formation (the offspring develop inside a mother-cell compartment using sporulation-related machinery, but never become dormant spores). Offspring grow through the day until they fill the mother cytoplasm, then mature cells emerge from the mother-cell envelope.
- **Numbers:** A mother produces 2 to 12 non-dormant intracellular offspring. Genome copies per cell are extreme: sources give "tens of thousands", "over 100,000", and "over 10,000" — all agree the cell is massively polyploid (Mendell et al. 2008, *PNAS* 105:6730–6734). Roughly 1% of mother-cell DNA is estimated to be passed to daughters. Cells reach 600 µm or more in length.
- **Energy and cost:** Maintaining tens of thousands of genome copies is a huge nucleotide and replication cost, but it is what lets a cell this large supply local transcription everywhere in its enormous volume.
- **What triggers it:** A daily cycle entrained to the host fish's feeding rhythm.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Utterly dependent on a specific gut habitat; binary fission has never been observed in type B, so there is no fallback mode. It has never been grown in pure culture.
- **Confidence:** High for the reproduction mode and giant size; medium for exact genome-copy numbers, which differ between sources.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2373351 ; https://cals.cornell.edu/microbiology/research/active-research-labs/angert-lab/epulopiscium

### 21. Budding and mother-cell ageing
- **Category:** Reproduction and division
- **Organism:** *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* (budding yeast)
- **What it does:** A yeast cell reproduces by growing a bud on its side and pinching it off, leaving a permanent scar. Each mother can only do this a limited number of times before she stops and dies — but every daughter is born young again.
- **How it works:** Division is asymmetric: damage, including protein aggregates and extrachromosomal rDNA circles, is preferentially retained by the mother. Each bud leaves a chitin-rich bud scar, which is how generations are counted. Replicative lifespan is measured by microdissecting daughters away from a mother and counting divisions until senescence.
- **Numbers:** Median replicative lifespan around 26 generations in a haploid S288C background and 36 generations in a diploid Mother Enrichment Program strain; a review gives a typical mean near 25 generations with a maximum near 40. Bud-scar labelling distinguishes young cells (about 5.5 divisions) from old (about 16.7).
- **Energy and cost:** Asymmetric damage retention costs the mother her future in exchange for rejuvenated daughters — a trade-off, not a defect.
- **What triggers it:** Nutrient sufficiency triggers budding; senescence arrives as damage accumulates.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Chitin itself is not the cause: inducing chitin deposition in the cell wall had no appreciable effect on lifespan, so the scar is a counter, not a clock. Lifespan values vary a lot by strain and assay.
- **Confidence:** High for asymmetric ageing; medium for any single median lifespan number.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2766306 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC209550

### 22. Burst into dozens (schizogony)
- **Category:** Reproduction and division
- **Organism:** *Plasmodium falciparum* (apicomplexan; malaria)
- **What it does:** Inside a red blood cell, the parasite copies its DNA several times without dividing, then carves itself up all at once into a batch of new parasites which burst out together and invade fresh cells. In the liver, the same trick produces tens of thousands at a go.
- **How it works:** Schizogony: repeated rounds of nuclear division within a shared cytoplasm, followed by a single synchronous segmentation in which each nucleus is packaged with organelles into a daughter merozoite, then egress by rupture of the parasitophorous vacuole and host cell membranes.
- **Numbers:** Blood-stage schizonts contain about 8–32 merozoites (one source reports up to 36), on a roughly 48-hour cycle in *P. falciparum*. Liver-stage schizonts yield on the order of 30,000–40,000 merozoites after about 5.5–6 days. These figures come largely from secondary sources and should be checked against primary literature before being treated as precise.
- **Energy and cost:** Everything is paid for with host haemoglobin and nutrients; the cost to the parasite is the all-or-nothing timing — a batch that segments badly is lost entirely.
- **What triggers it:** Developmental timing within the host cell; egress is driven by a protease cascade.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Synchronous bursting is exactly what the immune system and fever respond to. Each merozoite has only minutes to find a new red cell before it is cleared.
- **Confidence:** High for the mechanism; medium for the precise merozoite counts given the source quality.
- **Sources:** https://www2.tulane.edu/~wiser/protozoology/notes/mal_lc.html ; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6162063/ (general parasitology context)

### 23. Shrink each generation, reset by sex
- **Category:** Reproduction and division
- **Organism:** Diatoms generally; studied in pennate diatoms such as *Achnanthes* and in *Seminavis*
- **What it does:** A diatom lives in a glass box with a lid. Each time it divides, one daughter builds a new, slightly smaller box inside the old lid — so the average cell in the population shrinks generation by generation. Below a threshold size, the cells become able to have sex, and the fertilised cell inflates into a giant that rebuilds a full-size box.
- **How it works:** Each new valve forms inside the parental theca, so mean frustule size declines across mitotic divisions — the MacDonald–Pfitzer rule. Falling below a species-specific sexual size threshold is the primary requirement for sexual competence; microsurgery showing that newborn cells shrunk below the threshold acquire competence demonstrates that size, not age or division count, is the trigger. The zygote expands into an auxospore, restoring maximal size.
- **Numbers:** Sex typically occurs when cell size has fallen to roughly 50–20% of the maximal size (a rough figure resting on a single citation). Cultures can lose viability once average size falls below a critical value.
- **Energy and cost:** The silica box is cheap compared with organic walls, but the shrinking rule makes sex obligatory on a multi-year timescale, with all the costs of finding a partner.
- **What triggers it:** Falling below the species-specific size threshold, plus mating-type encounter and often light and nutrient cues.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Not universal: the rule does not apply to all taxa (*Eunotia minus* is a known exception). Sexual events are rarely observed in nature, so the field-level dynamics are uncertain.
- **Confidence:** High for the rule and the size threshold; medium for the 50–20% figure.
- **Sources:** https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/0967026042000236445 ; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2021.793665/pdf

### 24. Grow big, split into 2^n
- **Category:** Reproduction and division
- **Organism:** *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii* (green alga)
- **What it does:** Rather than doubling, this alga spends the day growing many times bigger in the light, then at night divides several times in a row without growing, producing 4, 8 or 16 small daughters at once — the number depending on how big it got.
- **How it works:** A long G1 is followed by n rounds of alternating S/M, yielding 2^n daughters. A mid-G1 "sizer" sets the commitment point: CDKA1 (a CDK1 orthologue) sets the critical size for commitment, while the Rb/E2F/DP1 pathway (MAT3-DP1) controls division number as well as commitment size, so bigger mothers divide more times and daughter size stays roughly constant.
- **Numbers:** In constant light, one cycle is about 12 h with n = 3, giving 8 daughters. About 40% of daughters fell outside the 2-fold range a "perfect" sizer predicts, prompting a modified threshold model in which cells must exceed about twice their median starting size to commit; cells also show a bias against dividing just once.
- **Energy and cost:** Growth and division are temporally separated, so the photosynthetic machinery is not disrupted by mitosis during the light period — an efficiency gain paid for with tight size-control regulation.
- **What triggers it:** Attaining the commitment size, gated by the light–dark cycle.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Whether size alone suffices or a timer also contributes is still debated. The strategy depends on a reliable light cycle.
- **Confidence:** High for multiple fission and size control; medium for the precise sizer model.
- **Sources:** https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/648436.full.pdf ; https://doaj.org/article/5491074117b642299609975d25cc0b65

## 4. Mating, gene exchange and sex

### 25. Plasmid mating bridge (conjugation)
- **Category:** Mating, gene exchange and sex
- **Organism:** *Escherichia coli* carrying the F plasmid (bacterium)
- **What it does:** One bacterium extends a thin tube to another, pulls it close, and pushes a copy of a DNA ring through into it. The receiving cell becomes a donor too, so the DNA spreads through the population like a chain letter — this is how antibiotic resistance travels between species.
- **How it works:** The F plasmid encodes a type IV secretion system and an F pilus. After pilus-mediated contact and mating-pair stabilisation, a relaxase nicks the plasmid at oriT and a single strand is transferred 5'-first through the channel, with complementary strands synthesised in both cells. In Hfr strains the plasmid has integrated, so chromosomal genes are transferred in a fixed order over time — the basis of interrupted-mating genetic mapping.
- **Numbers:** F+ × F− crosses transfer the plasmid to roughly 50% of recipients within an hour; chromosomal transfer in such crosses is rare (10^-5 to 10^-7 per donor). Transfer of the entire *E. coli* chromosome takes about 100 minutes at a roughly constant rate (a textbook figure of about 1,000 nucleotides per second), but mating pairs almost always break before completion.
- **Energy and cost:** Building a pilus and running a secretion ATPase is costly, and the plasmid itself imposes a replication and expression burden. Pili are also receptors for male-specific phages, so being a donor attracts infection.
- **What triggers it:** Contact with a recipient lacking the plasmid; in many systems transfer genes are repressed and de-repressed only transiently after entering a new host.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Mating pairs are fragile — shearing stops transfer. Surface exclusion blocks redundant transfer into cells that already have the plasmid.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Sources:** https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Cell_and_Molecular_Biology/Biofundamentals_1e_(Klymkowsky_and_Cooper)/09%3A_Genomes_genes_and_regulatory_networks/9.6%3A_Conjugation ; https://collections.nlm.nih.gov/ocr/nlm:nlmuid-101584906X19325-doc

### 26. Pheromone-triggered DNA uptake
- **Category:** Mating, gene exchange and sex
- **Organism:** *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** When enough of these bacteria are together, they shout a short chemical word at each other. Hearing it, they all switch into a state where they can pull naked DNA out of the water and paste it into their own genome — and they kill their non-shouting relatives to get it.
- **How it works:** The *comC* gene encodes a secreted competence-stimulating peptide (CSP). CSP binds the surface histidine kinase ComD, which autophosphorylates and transfers phosphate to the response regulator ComE, switching on the competence regulon: DNA-uptake machinery plus, separately, killing factors. "Fratricide" — CSP-regulated proteins not needed for transformation itself lyse non-competent pneumococci, releasing their DNA for the competent cells to take up. CibAB is a related peptide system that acts directly rather than through a receptor.
- **Numbers:** No reliable figure verified here for CSP threshold concentration or transformation frequency.
- **Energy and cost:** Competence costs a large regulon's worth of protein and a temporary growth pause, plus the destruction of close relatives. The payoff is new genes, including the beta-lactam resistance determinants that have spread this way.
- **What triggers it:** Accumulating CSP (population density and confinement); also stress and some antibiotics.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Competence is transient — a short window only. Taking up DNA risks importing phage or deleterious sequences. Strains carry different CSP "pherotypes" that do not cross-activate.
- **Confidence:** High for the CSP/ComDE circuit and fratricide; medium for the detailed activation mechanism of ComD, which is acknowledged to be poorly understood.
- **Sources:** https://academic.oup.com/femsre/article/33/3/627/591065 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5295010

### 27. Kill neighbours, steal their DNA
- **Category:** Mating, gene exchange and sex
- **Organism:** *Vibrio cholerae* (bacterium) on chitin surfaces
- **What it does:** Living on a crab shell, this bacterium switches on both a spear gun and a DNA-grabbing machine at the same time. It stabs neighbouring bacteria, they burst, and it sucks up their genes.
- **How it works:** The T6SS gene cluster belongs to the competence regulon and is positively controlled by the competence regulators TfoX and QstR; growth on chitinous surfaces induces both. Live-cell imaging showed that killing non-immune cells by competence-induced T6SS activity releases DNA that is then taken up for horizontal gene transfer. Predatory strains killed *E. coli* and other *V. cholerae* strains.
- **Numbers:** The competence-linked T6SS comprises about 13 components. No reliable figure verified for transfer efficiency.
- **Energy and cost:** Pays for both an expensive weapon and an expensive uptake system at once; the payoff is that the DNA is fresh, local and from organisms that survive the same habitat.
- **What triggers it:** Chitin as a surface and nutrient; quorum and competence regulators TfoX and QstR.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Sister cells with matching immunity proteins cannot be harvested. Contact-dependent, so it needs a crowded surface.
- **Confidence:** High (Borgeaud et al. 2015, *Science* 347:63–67).
- **Sources:** https://infoscience.epfl.ch/items/f2423201-c065-4620-a9a8-4681e8b9eca3 ; https://actu.epfl.ch/news/killing-for-dna-a-predatory-device-in-the-choler-3

### 28. Gene-delivery capsules (GTA)
- **Category:** Mating, gene exchange and sex
- **Organism:** *Rhodobacter capsulatus* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** These bacteria build what look like tiny viruses — but the particles never carry the genes for making more particles. Instead each one is packed with a random chunk of the producer's own genome, and delivers it to another cell. It is a virus repurposed as a postal service.
- **How it works:** The RcGTA particle resembles a small tailed bacteriophage, with a capsid around a roughly 4 kb linear DNA fragment consisting of random segments of the *R. capsulatus* genome. Because the capsid is too small to hold the whole GTA gene cluster, a particle cannot propagate itself — transfer is strictly to the benefit of the recipient's gene pool. Particles are released as cultures enter stationary phase.
- **Numbers:** Packaged fragment about 4 kb — much smaller than the gene cluster that encodes the particle, which is why self-propagation is impossible.
- **Energy and cost:** Producing phage-like particles is expensive and, in at least some systems, lethal to the producing cell, with no direct benefit to the producer — an unusual case where the trait's maintenance is genuinely puzzling.
- **What triggers it:** Entry into stationary phase; quorum and regulatory control.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Only a small genome fragment fits per particle, so transfer is piecemeal. Whether release is by lysis is not firmly established in the sources consulted.
- **Confidence:** Medium. The particle and its random cargo are well documented; the evolutionary reason it persists, and the exact release mechanism, remain debated.
- **Sources:** https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s002030100260 ; https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/213070

### 29. Seven sexes
- **Category:** Mating, gene exchange and sex
- **Organism:** *Tetrahymena thermophila* (ciliate)
- **What it does:** This pond ciliate has seven mating types instead of two, and it can pair with any of the other six. When two starved cells of different types meet, they fuse partially, swap half-genomes, and each rebuilds a brand-new working nucleus from scratch.
- **How it works:** Each cell has a silent germline micronucleus and an active somatic macronucleus. Mating-type determination requires complex DNA rearrangements at a single *mat* locus during conjugation, with one type stochastically and irreversibly expressed in each new somatic nucleus. In conjugation the micronucleus undergoes meiosis to four haploid nuclei, three degenerate, the survivor divides mitotically into two pronuclei, pronuclei are exchanged and fuse, the parental macronucleus is destroyed and a new one is built from the zygotic nucleus.
- **Numbers:** Seven mating types (I–VII), giving 21 compatible pairings; a cell cannot mate with its own type. Conjugation takes about 12 hours at 30 °C, longer when cooler. Starvation for at least 2 h is needed before pairing.
- **Energy and cost:** Conjugation halts feeding and growth for half a day and destroys the existing somatic nucleus — a complete, risky rebuild of the working genome.
- **What triggers it:** Starvation plus encounter with a complementary mating type.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Requires a partner of a different type; the whole process is slow and leaves the cell non-growing while it happens.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahymena_thermophila ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3608545

### 30. Cell-fusion mating
- **Category:** Mating, gene exchange and sex
- **Organism:** *Haloferax volcanii* (archaeon; salt-loving)
- **What it does:** Two of these archaea recognise each other, build a bridge between their bodies, and merge their insides — not a narrow tube for DNA like bacterial conjugation, but a genuine shared cytoplasm. Both chromosomes and plasmids move in both directions, and the cells then separate again.
- **How it works:** After cell–cell recognition, bridges form and expand, allowing two-way exchange of plasmids and chromosomes and producing heterodiploid cells, which later resolve into recombinants. Cryo-electron tomography of mating pairs showed a continuous membrane, a continuous S-layer and connected cytosols, with macromolecular complexes such as ribosomes inside the bridges, so cellular components as well as DNA can cross.
- **Numbers:** Fusion efficiency is about 815-fold higher within *Haloferax* species than between species.
- **Energy and cost:** Merging and then re-separating two cells is a major membrane and S-layer remodelling job, with the risk that genome mixing produces an unviable combination.
- **What triggers it:** Cell–cell contact on solid media; conditions promoting aggregation.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Strongly biased towards same-species partners; the molecular machinery driving fusion is not yet identified.
- **Confidence:** High for the fused-cytoplasm observation (cryo-ET, 2020/2021); medium for the detailed exchange model.
- **Sources:** https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7838353/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3669151

### 31. UV-alarm DNA swap
- **Category:** Mating, gene exchange and sex
- **Organism:** *Sulfolobus solfataricus*, *S. acidocaldarius* (hyperthermophilic archaea)
- **What it does:** When sunlight or chemicals shatter its DNA, this archaeon grows sticky hairs, clumps together with its own species, and swaps chromosome pieces with its neighbours. It uses the borrowed copies as templates to repair the damage.
- **How it works:** UV induces the *ups* operon, producing straight type IV pili about 10 nm wide, built from UpsA/B pre-pilins and requiring the secretion ATPase SSO0120. Pili mediate species-specific cellular aggregation; within aggregates, chromosomal markers are exchanged and used for homologous recombination repair. Aggregation is dose-dependent on UV, is not triggered by pH or temperature shifts, and is stimulated by chemically induced double-strand breaks.
- **Numbers:** Recombination rates in UV-induced cultures were up to three orders of magnitude higher than in uninduced cultures. Pilus knockout strains had reduced survival after UV.
- **Energy and cost:** Pilus assembly and aggregation halt dispersal and cost ATP, but the alternative is unrepaired double-strand breaks.
- **What triggers it:** UV irradiation and other DNA double-strand-break-inducing agents.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Needs nearby conspecifics — a lone cell cannot use it. Species-specific recognition limits the donor pool.
- **Confidence:** High (Fröls et al. 2008; Ajon et al. 2011, *Mol Microbiol* 82:807–817).
- **Sources:** https://archaea.univie.ac.at/publications/publications-detail/pure/6605936d-ca27-4114-a9dc-b44442a5a5d8/show/publ/Pure ; https://research.rug.nl/nl/publications/uv-inducible-cellular-aggregation-of-the-hyperthermophilic-archae/

### 32. Cannibal giant zygote
- **Category:** Mating, gene exchange and sex
- **Organism:** *Dictyostelium discoideum* (social amoeba)
- **What it does:** Two amoebae of different mating types fuse into one giant cell. The giant then calls in hundreds more amoebae with a chemical signal — and eats them. The survivors wall themselves in around it, forming a hard cyst in which the giant's genome later recombines and splits into many new amoebae.
- **How it works:** Amoebae aggregate to cAMP and sex pheromones; two cells of opposite mating types fuse to form a giant zygote which secretes large quantities of cAMP, drawing in peripheral cells. Some are engulfed by phagocytosis; the rest produce a cellulose wall, forming the macrocyst. Inside, the giant cell undergoes meiosis and then mitosis to produce many haploid amoebae.
- **Numbers:** A macrocyst can contain thousands of cannibalised cells; because chemotaxis is faster than phagocytosis, the zygote becomes surrounded by hundreds of cells as a precyst aggregate. Three mating types; isogamous cells of any two can fuse.
- **Energy and cost:** The recruited amoebae pay with their lives and are converted into the zygote's food store and wall. For the zygote this is an exceptionally cheap way to fund a long dormancy.
- **What triggers it:** Darkness, high humidity, starvation and the presence of a compatible mating type.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Germination of macrocysts is very rare under laboratory conditions, so the full cycle is hard to study. An earlier report that macrocysts form without a compatible partner was not reproduced.
- **Confidence:** High for the cycle as described; low for germination control and for self-macrocyst formation.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictyostelium_discoideum ; https://ijdb.ehu.eus/article/pdf/190183gb

## 5. Resource management

### 33. Night-shift nitrogen fixing
- **Category:** Resource management
- **Organism:** *Crocosphaera watsonii* (unicellular marine cyanobacterium)
- **What it does:** This cell does two jobs that cannot be done at the same time: photosynthesis, which makes oxygen, and nitrogen fixing, which oxygen destroys. It solves the clash with a shift pattern — photosynthesis by day, nitrogen fixing at night — and even shuffles its scarce iron between the two machines.
- **How it works:** Proteomics shows nitrogen-fixation proteins peaking at night and photosynthesis proteins at midday. Iron moves into photosystems during the day and into nitrogenase after sunset, then back before dawn; the nitrogenase complex is broken down and rebuilt each day. Photosystem II shuts down at night — unusual among phototrophs — giving nitrogenase extra protection from oxygen. Daytime photosynthesis builds starch reserves that power night-time fixation.
- **Numbers:** No reliable figure verified here for ATP per N2 fixed in this organism (nitrogenase in general is famously expensive, but I did not confirm a species-specific value).
- **Energy and cost:** The cell destroys and rebuilds a major enzyme complex every 24 hours and keeps its iron in constant transit — a large recurring protein and metal-handling cost, accepted because iron is scarce in the open ocean.
- **What triggers it:** The day–night cycle; the schedule is under circadian and light control.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** The separation weakens when the dark period is short, and some conditions allow fixation in the light. The whole strategy depends on reliable day length.
- **Confidence:** High for temporal separation and PSII shutdown; medium for the iron-shuttling model, which rests on a modelling plus proteomics argument.
- **Sources:** https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12265555/ ; https://hal.sorbonne-universite.fr/hal-01263684/document

### 34. Phosphate bank
- **Category:** Resource management
- **Organism:** *Candidatus* Accumulibacter phosphatis (bacterium; sewage-treatment polyphosphate accumulator)
- **What it does:** This bacterium hoards phosphate inside itself as long chains, like a battery. When the oxygen goes away and food arrives, it spends the battery to grab the food; when oxygen returns it recharges, taking far more phosphate out of the water than it needs. Sewage plants exploit this to strip phosphorus from wastewater.
- **How it works:** In the anaerobic "feast" phase the cell hydrolyses stored polyphosphate (releasing phosphate) and uses the energy, plus glycogen, to take up volatile fatty acids and store them as polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA). In the aerobic "famine" phase it oxidises PHA to grow and to replenish glycogen and polyphosphate, taking up more phosphate than it released.
- **Numbers:** No reliable per-cell figures verified here; the effect is defined operationally by net phosphorus removal in reactors.
- **Energy and cost:** Polyphosphate is both an energy store and a phosphate store; maintaining it ties up a large fraction of cell volume and requires strict alternation of conditions.
- **What triggers it:** The imposed anaerobic–aerobic cycle of the treatment process; the same alternation occurs in some natural sediments.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Performance is unstable in full-scale plants, apparently driven by shifts in redox conditions. The organism can also behave like a glycogen-accumulating organism, abandoning phosphate uptake, which complicates the literature. The source of reducing power for anaerobic PHA accumulation remains unresolved.
- **Confidence:** Medium. The cycle is well established; clade-level physiology and the reducing-power question are open.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5148189 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4872125

### 35. Stolen solar panels plus stolen nucleus
- **Category:** Resource management
- **Organism:** *Mesodinium rubrum* (ciliate) stealing from *Geminigera cryophila* (cryptophyte alga)
- **What it does:** This ciliate eats algae, then keeps their chloroplasts alive inside itself and runs them like solar panels. It goes one better than other thieves: it also keeps the algal cell's nucleus, still switched on, so the stolen machinery keeps being maintained instead of running down.
- **How it works:** Kleptoplasty plus a retained, transcriptionally active but non-dividing prey nucleus — the "kleptokaryon" — that directs plastid activity, and is implicated in plastid division and photoacclimation. Transcriptome comparisons show major transcriptional changes in the stolen nucleus relative to free-living cells, and suggest it also controls some pathways of the host's metabolism.
- **Numbers:** No reliable figure verified here for plastid retention time or photosynthetic contribution in this species.
- **Energy and cost:** Far cheaper than evolving and encoding a plastid: the ciliate gets photosynthesis without carrying thousands of plastid-support genes. The cost is dependence on repeated capture of the right prey.
- **What triggers it:** Feeding on cryptophyte prey.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** The stolen nucleus does not divide, so the arrangement expires and must be renewed by eating more algae. It works only with specific prey.
- **Confidence:** High that the nucleus is retained and active (Johnson et al. 2007, *Nature* 445:426–428, and follow-up transcriptomics); medium on how much host metabolism it controls.
- **Sources:** https://dx.doi.org/10.1038/nature05496 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4609049

### 36. Light-powered proton pump
- **Category:** Resource management
- **Organism:** Uncultivated SAR86 gammaproteobacteria and many other marine microbes (bacteria, archaea, protists)
- **What it does:** Ordinary ocean bacteria that are not "plants" still run partly on sunlight. A single protein in the membrane catches a photon and uses it to push a proton out of the cell, charging the membrane like a battery that the cell can spend on making ATP or moving.
- **How it works:** Proteorhodopsin is a seven-transmembrane protein binding retinal. Light isomerises the retinal, driving a conformational cycle that translocates a proton across the membrane, contributing to the proton motive force. It was discovered by cloning a 130-kb genomic fragment from an uncultivated marine gammaproteobacterium and expressing the gene in *E. coli*, where it formed an active light-driven proton pump.
- **Numbers:** An estimated ~13% of bacteria near the ocean surface carry proteorhodopsin; since 2000 PRs have been detected in numerous bacteria, archaea and microbial eukaryotes.
- **Energy and cost:** Extremely cheap for what it does: one small protein plus a retinal cofactor, with no reaction centres, antenna complexes or carbon fixation machinery. It supplements rather than replaces heterotrophy.
- **What triggers it:** Light; expression is often regulated by light and by nutrient limitation.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** It makes proton motive force, not fixed carbon, so the cell still needs organic food. Benefits are hard to demonstrate in culture, and whether it helps SAR11 (*Pelagibacter*) survive starvation was not verified here.
- **Confidence:** High that it is a light-driven proton pump and is widespread; medium on its quantitative contribution to any given cell's energy budget.
- **Sources:** https://hahana.soest.hawaii.edu/cmoreserver/summercourse/2014/documents/DeLong_2/beja_sci_rhodop.pdf ; https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/proteorhodopsins-widespread-microbial-light-driven-proton-pumps-w/

### 37. Rocket-fuel metabolism (anammox)
- **Category:** Resource management
- **Organism:** *Candidatus* Brocadia, *Ca.* Kuenenia, *Ca.* Scalindua (bacteria)
- **What it does:** These bacteria make a living with no oxygen and no sunlight by reacting ammonium with nitrite to make nitrogen gas. Along the way they produce hydrazine — the stuff used as rocket fuel — and they keep it safely inside a sealed compartment so it does not poison them. They grow so slowly that a population takes a week or more to double.
- **How it works:** The reactions happen inside the anammoxosome, a membrane-bound organelle whose membrane is built largely from ladderane lipids — concatenated cyclobutane rings that make it unusually dense and nearly impermeable to hydrazine and protons. Hydrazine is a free intermediate; the proton gradient maintained across the anammoxosome membrane drives ATP synthesis.
- **Numbers:** Doubling time commonly quoted as about 11 days due to low substrate conversion rate; a later study estimated 1.8 days, which those authors considered unlikely. Other sources give 7–22 days or 1–2 weeks. Five genera are described: *Brocadia*, *Kuenenia*, *Anammoxoglobus*, *Jettenia* (freshwater) and *Scalindua* (marine).
- **Energy and cost:** The energy yield per reaction is small, which is why growth is so slow; much of the budget goes to the specialised lipid membrane that contains a toxic intermediate.
- **What triggers it:** Anoxic conditions with both ammonium and nitrite available.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Extreme slow growth makes the cells easy to wash out of a reactor and slow to recover; they are inhibited by oxygen and by excess nitrite.
- **Confidence:** High for the chemistry and the compartment; medium for doubling time, which is disputed.
- **Sources:** https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Microbiology/Microbiology_(Boundless)/05%3A_Microbial_Metabolism/5.10%3A_Chemolithotrophy/5.10F%3A_Anammox ; https://academic.oup.com/femsec/article/63/1/46/491222

### 38. Nitrate scuba tank
- **Category:** Resource management
- **Organism:** *Thiomargarita namibiensis* (bacterium; "sulfur pearl of Namibia")
- **What it does:** This bacterium is almost all storage tank. A single enormous bubble inside it holds concentrated nitrate, which it breathes while eating the toxic sulfide in the mud around it. The tank is big enough to last for months between refills.
- **How it works:** A vast liquid vacuole fills most of the cell, with the active cytoplasm confined to a thin outer shell — which solves the diffusion problem that normally limits bacterial size. Stored nitrate is used as the electron acceptor for oxidising hydrogen sulfide to elemental sulfur, which is deposited as the white granules that give the chains their pearly look.
- **Numbers:** Cells average 100–300 µm diameter, reaching 750 µm; the vacuole occupies about 98% of the biovolume; stored nitrate is reported at up to about 0.8 M (800 mM) — a figure that appears in species summaries rather than verified from the 1999 paper's own text. Schulz et al. 1999, *Science* 284:493–495.
- **Energy and cost:** Storage is the whole strategy: the cell pays in volume and in the cost of concentrating nitrate roughly ten-thousand-fold above seawater, buying independence from a wildly fluctuating supply.
- **What triggers it:** Nitrate availability during resuspension events; sulfide availability in the sediment.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Essentially immobile and dependent on sediment disturbance to refill. Enormous size makes it a conspicuous, slow organism.
- **Confidence:** High for size and vacuole; medium for the 0.8 M figure.
- **Sources:** https://bionumbers.hms.harvard.edu/bionumber.aspx?id=102207 ; https://www.mpi-bremen.de/en/Press-release-1999.html

### 39. Breathe metal through wires
- **Category:** Resource management
- **Organism:** *Geobacter sulfurreducens* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** This bacterium breathes rust. Because rust is a solid and cannot be brought inside the cell, the bacterium grows conductive protein filaments and pushes its waste electrons down them into the mineral — effectively plugging itself into the ground.
- **How it works:** Electrons from metabolism reach the cell surface through a chain of multi-haem cytochromes, then travel along extracellular filaments to the mineral or electrode. What the filaments are made of is disputed: one camp holds that type IV pili ("e-pili") are the conductors; another, based on cryo-EM, argues that the filaments are polymers of the cytochrome OmcS (and OmcZ), and that earlier "pili" preparations were misidentified.
- **Numbers:** e-pili are about 3 nm in diameter; heterologous PilA experiments reported conductivity differences of more than 1,000-fold at that same diameter. Deleting *omcS* in strain KN400 did not reduce Fe(III) oxide reduction and cells still expressed conductive filaments; in other work, deleting *omcS* increased biofilm conductivity.
- **Energy and cost:** Electron transfer to a distant solid acceptor wastes some of the available redox span and requires large amounts of cytochrome protein, which is iron-expensive.
- **What triggers it:** Growth with an insoluble electron acceptor (Fe(III) oxide or an electrode) and no soluble alternative.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Works only in contact with or near the acceptor; slow compared with soluble-acceptor respiration.
- **Confidence:** Medium that long-range extracellular electron transfer occurs (high, in fact); **low** on which filament is the primary conductor — this is an active and sharp dispute between the e-pili and cytochrome-filament camps, with a middle-ground proposal that pili are structural and OmcS/OmcZ conductive.
- **Sources:** https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6771412/ ; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9239076/

### 40. Carry your own crop (farming)
- **Category:** Resource management
- **Organism:** *Dictyostelium discoideum* (social amoeba)
- **What it does:** Some wild strains of this amoeba stop eating before the food runs out, pack the surviving bacteria into their spore mass, and carry them to the next place. When the spores land, the amoebae seed a new bacterial crop and eat that.
- **How it works:** "Farmer" clones cease feeding early and incorporate bacteria into their fruiting bodies, dispersing them with the spores and seeding a new food crop at the new site. Farmer clones also carry defensive bacteria whose secreted compounds harm non-farming amoebae; in one clone, the edible bacterium was later shown to have evolved from the toxic one.
- **Numbers:** About one-third of wild-collected clones engage in husbandry of bacteria (Brock et al. 2011, *Nature* 469:393–396).
- **Energy and cost:** The farmer gives up food it could have eaten now. That cost is only repaid if it lands somewhere with no suitable bacteria — which is why the trait is dichotomous rather than universal.
- **What triggers it:** Appears to be a heritable clone-level strategy rather than an induced response.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** If it arrives at a site that already has appropriate bacteria, the cost of early feeding cessation is not compensated. Whether "agriculture" is the right word for this is contested.
- **Confidence:** Medium. The carriage and dispersal are replicated; the framing as agriculture, and the stability of the symbiosis, are debated.
- **Sources:** https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/110119-amoebas-smallest-farmers-discodeum-science-nature-bacteria ; https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/primitive-agriculture-in-a-social-amoeba/

## 6. Movement and travel

### 41. Rotary flagellar motor
- **Category:** Movement and travel
- **Organism:** *Escherichia coli*; *Vibrio alginolyticus* (bacteria)
- **What it does:** The cell has a genuine rotating propeller, a stiff corkscrew spun by a motor embedded in its membrane. The motor runs on ions flowing inwards rather than on fuel, can reverse direction in a fraction of a second, and is the fastest-spinning rotor known in biology.
- **How it works:** Stator units (MotA/MotB in *E. coli*, PomA/PomB in *Vibrio*) conduct H+ (or Na+) down the electrochemical gradient and apply torque to the rotor C-ring; the rotor drives a helical flagellar filament through a hook. Switching between counter-clockwise and clockwise is controlled by CheY-P binding to FliM in the switch complex.
- **Numbers:** About 1,100–1,240 protons per revolution in *Streptococcus*, with ~1,200 used for both *Streptococcus* and *E. coli* in later modelling; estimated energy conversion efficiency about 0.9 at tethered-cell speeds. Torque is roughly constant at low speed then falls sharply past a "knee". *E. coli*/*Salmonella* proton motors run around 200–300 revolutions per second (bundle rotation in swimming cells on the order of 100 Hz); the *Vibrio alginolyticus* sodium motor reaches 1,700 revolutions per second at 300 mM NaCl and 35 °C — the fastest rotation recorded for any molecular motor.
- **Energy and cost:** Paid for in proton (or sodium) motive force rather than ATP directly: at ~1,200 protons per turn and ~100 turns per second, swimming is a large share of a small cell's energy budget. Building a flagellum requires dozens of genes and export of tens of thousands of subunits.
- **What triggers it:** Runs continuously when the cell is motile; direction is set by chemotaxis signalling.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Useless without a proton gradient, so it fails first when energy drops. Flagellin is a potent immune trigger in hosts, and flagella are phage receptors.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Sources:** https://bionumbers.hms.harvard.edu/bionumber.aspx?id=109759 ; https://groups.physics.ox.ac.uk/molecularmotors/pdfs%20of%20papers/QRB%20Sowa%20BFM%20review%202008.pdf

### 42. Grappling-hook crawl (twitching)
- **Category:** Movement and travel
- **Organism:** *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** The cell throws out long sticky filaments, catches hold of a surface, then pulls them back in, dragging itself forward. Each filament pulls with a force enormous for the cell's size, and several working together can pull harder still.
- **How it works:** Type IV pili extend by polymerising pilin subunits and retract when the ATPase PilT depolymerises them, probably through a cooperative conformational change in an oligomer. Retraction of the filament against an anchored tip generates the pulling force.
- **Numbers:** Single-pilus stall force 110 ± 30 pN; retraction velocity constant at low force and falling above about 40 pN. Bundled pili retracting cooperatively generate nanonewton-scale forces (Biais et al. 2008), with one filament exerting 50–100 pN. Reducing PilT levels and applying about 110 ± 10 pN of external force induces processive pilus elongation instead — a force-dependent switch.
- **Energy and cost:** ATP-driven depolymerisation; the pilin subunits are recycled rather than discarded, but the machinery is a large multi-protein assembly. Pili are also the organism's main antigen and phage/receptor liability.
- **What triggers it:** Surface contact; pili also mediate attachment, DNA uptake and aggregation.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Only works on surfaces, not in open water, and is slow compared with swimming.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Sources:** https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/single-pilus-motor-forces-exceed-100-pn/ ; https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article/info:doi/10.1371/journal.pbio.0060087

### 43. Conveyor-belt gliding
- **Category:** Movement and travel
- **Organism:** *Flavobacterium johnsoniae* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** This bacterium has no flagellum and no legs, yet it glides smoothly over surfaces. It runs a loop of sticky protein around the outside of its own body like a caterpillar track: the protein grips the surface, the track moves, and the cell screws itself forward.
- **How it works:** A rotary motor in the inner membrane, built from the type IX secretion proteins GldK/GldL/GldM/GldN, drives movement of the surface adhesin SprB along a closed helical track on the cell surface. Traction against the substrate makes the cell rotate about its long axis and advance in a screw-like motion. The machinery is powered by the proton gradient but not by the membrane electrical potential.
- **Numbers:** Cells glide at about 1.7–2 µm/s (some reports 2–4 µm/s); SprB filaments are about 160 nm long and beads attached to SprB move at roughly 2 µm/s across the surface; tethered cells rotate at about 1 Hz, and the motor appears to run at constant speed rather than constant torque. Cells lacking GldK/L/M/N are non-motile.
- **Energy and cost:** Proton motive force plus continuous production and surface trafficking of a large adhesin, with a dedicated secretion system that doubles as the motor.
- **What triggers it:** Contact with a surface.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Needs a surface to grip, and the machinery is also the cell's secretion system, so motility and secretion compete.
- **Confidence:** High for the rotary-motor-and-track model; medium for exact speeds.
- **Sources:** https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037e-6e22-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content ; https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.10.19.464928.full.pdf

### 44. Thousand-leg walking
- **Category:** Movement and travel
- **Organism:** *Mycoplasma mobile* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** A bacterium with no cell wall and no flagellum walks across surfaces on hundreds of molecular legs. Each leg grabs a sugar on the surface, pulls, lets go and reaches forward again — a genuine stepping gait at the scale of single proteins.
- **How it works:** The gliding machinery at one pole carries Gli349 "legs", rods about 95 nm long with flexible hinges, whose tips bind sialylated oligosaccharides on the substrate. The cycle of binding, stroke and release is driven by ATP hydrolysis in an internal motor, as shown by reactivating permeabilised "ghost" cells with ATP.
- **Numbers:** Gliding speed 2.0–4.5 µm/s (reviews give 2.5–4 µm/s for *M. mobile*, 0.5–1 µm/s for *M. pneumoniae*). Ghost experiments under reduced motor interaction revealed regular steps of about 70 nm. A preprint estimates a step angle of 34°, stepping torque 85–120 pN·nm, and a rate-limiting rate of 15.5 s^-1; high-speed AFM saw particles moving about 9 nm sideways and 2 nm inward over 330 ms, dependent on ATP.
- **Energy and cost:** ATP per step. The organism is a minimal parasite with a tiny genome, and the gliding machinery is one of the few large structures it retains, which suggests high value.
- **What triggers it:** Contact with a sialylated surface.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Only works on surfaces bearing the right sugars — essentially host tissue. No wall means the cell is fragile.
- **Confidence:** High for the leg-and-step mechanism; medium for the torque and rate estimates, which come from a preprint.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6068192 ; https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.01.28.428740v1.full.pdf

### 45. Gas-filled floats
- **Category:** Movement and travel
- **Organism:** *Planktothrix* spp., *Anabaena* spp. (cyanobacteria); also *Haloquadratum*
- **What it does:** These cells build hollow protein balloons full of gas inside themselves. The balloons make the cell lighter than water so it floats up to the light, and the cell can sink again by loading itself with heavy sugar. It is a buoyancy tank with no moving parts.
- **How it works:** Gas vesicles are hollow cylinders with conical ends, built from the small protein GvpA with the external scaffold GvpC; the 2 nm-thick shell is hydrophobic inside and hydrophilic outside, so gas diffuses in freely but liquid water cannot enter. Buoyancy is regulated on a daily cycle by accumulating dense carbohydrate ballast during photosynthesis.
- **Numbers:** Removing GvpC cuts the critical collapse pressure roughly threefold. Width and strength trade off inversely: *Planktothrix* isolates from lakes of different depths had widths of about 51, 58 and 67 nm with collapse pressures of 1.1, 0.9 and 0.7 MPa. Hydrostatic pressure rises about 0.01 MPa per metre of depth.
- **Energy and cost:** Wider vesicles provide buoyancy at lower energetic cost but collapse at lower pressure, so each species buys depth tolerance with material. Collapse is irreversible and rebuilding is expensive.
- **What triggers it:** Constitutive synthesis with regulation by light and depth; ballast accumulation is driven by photosynthesis.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Crushed by pressure below the species' limit, and a cell driven too deep can never come back up. Floating cells are stranded at the surface in calm weather and can be killed by excess light.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Sources:** https://bmcmicrobiol.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12866-020-01805-8 ; https://oceanrep.geomar.de/id/eprint/29767/1/Walsby%20et%20al_1995-1.pdf

### 46. ATP-powered archaeal propeller
- **Category:** Movement and travel
- **Organism:** *Halobacterium salinarum* (archaeon)
- **What it does:** Archaea also swim with a rotating propeller, but theirs is a completely different machine from the bacterial one — it is built like a type IV pilus and it burns ATP directly rather than running on an ion flow.
- **How it works:** The archaellum is a rotating filament of archaellins driven by a motor whose core is the ATPase FlaI, an assembly ATPase of the type IV pilus family. Rotation appears to proceed in discrete steps, each plausibly coupled to one ATP hydrolysis event.
- **Numbers:** Tethered-cell assays showed intermittent pauses at roughly 36° or 60° intervals, suggesting unitary steps; motor torque was estimated at about 50 pN·nm with efficiency around 6–10% (Kinosita et al. 2016). Rotation speed about 23 ± 5 Hz by cross-kymography, and about 21.5–21.8 Hz by bead tracking with an upper limit near 25 Hz. A 2019 study put torque near 160 pN·nm and found work per rotation about twice that of six ATP; a 2020 ghost-bead study concluded at least 12 ATP are hydrolysed per revolution.
- **Energy and cost:** ATP directly — 12 or more molecules per revolution by the highest estimate — which is costly, but it frees the motor from needing a strong ion gradient.
- **What triggers it:** Motility signalling; archaea use a chemotaxis system homologous to the bacterial one despite the different motor.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Far slower than the bacterial flagellar motor. The torque estimates differ roughly threefold between studies, so the energetics are not settled.
- **Confidence:** High that it is an ATP-driven rotary motor; medium for torque and ATP-per-revolution.
- **Sources:** https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6534597/ ; https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.01.08.899351v2.full

### 47. Walking on leg-like cirri
- **Category:** Movement and travel
- **Organism:** *Euplotes eurystomus* (ciliate)
- **What it does:** This ciliate does not swim across surfaces — it walks, on about a dozen stiff bundles of fused cilia that work like legs. The sequence of steps is not a simple repeating rhythm; the cell moves between a small set of recognisable leg-states.
- **How it works:** Cirri are bundles of cilia acting as appendages. High-speed tracking showed that walking entails regulated transitions among a discrete set of gait states, with no periodicity: the state sequences are consistent with either stochastic processes or complex deterministic mechanisms. Later analysis described the gait as cyclic and stochastic with broken detailed balance. Perturbation experiments showed that internal fibres mediate gait coordination, suggesting a mechanical basis for gait control.
- **Numbers:** No reliable figure verified here for walking speed or step frequency.
- **Energy and cost:** Ciliary beating is ATP-expensive; walking on a surface likely costs less than swimming for a given displacement, but this was not verified.
- **What triggers it:** Contact with a surface; the cell switches between swimming and walking.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Caution on the framing: this is coordinated locomotion with discrete states, not evidence of a nervous system or decision-making. The claimed 2024 Laeverenz-Schlögelhofer and Wan paper could not be located; the verified work is Larson, Garbus, Pollack and Marshall, *Current Biology*, 2022.
- **Confidence:** Medium. The gait-state result is from one group and the underlying control mechanism is unresolved.
- **Sources:** https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.02.26.433123v4.full ; https://www.pubmed.gov/35963241/

### 48. Calcium-powered spring
- **Category:** Movement and travel
- **Organism:** *Vorticella convallaria* (ciliate)
- **What it does:** This bell-shaped ciliate lives on a stalk. When disturbed it yanks itself down in a few thousandths of a second, the stalk coiling like a released spring. It does this without using ATP at the moment of contraction — the trigger is calcium.
- **How it works:** The stalk contains the spasmoneme, a bundle of the Ca2+-binding protein spasmin. A calcium influx causes the protein assembly to collapse, releasing stored elastic energy. Energy is derived from the binding of calcium ions and not from the hydrolysis of ATP. Re-extension requires dissociation of Ca2+ from spasmin and active sequestration of calcium back into storage sites, which is where ATP is spent.
- **Numbers:** Maximum contraction velocity scales inversely with the square root of medium viscosity, implying the rate is limited by the power the spasmoneme delivers (Upadhyaya et al. 2008, *Biophys J* 94:265). Reported peak speeds differ: about 6 cm/s with tensile force up to 500 nN in one modelling paper, about 8 cm/s in a press account of other work.
- **Energy and cost:** The contraction itself is nearly free; the cost is front-loaded in building the spring and back-loaded in pumping calcium back. Press claims that it beats muscles and car engines gram for gram are not backed by a verified watt-per-kilogram figure.
- **What triggers it:** Mechanical disturbance or predator contact, via calcium release.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Single-shot until calcium is repumped; the cell cannot feed while contracted. Being sessile, it cannot flee.
- **Confidence:** High for the calcium-driven, ATP-free contraction; medium for the speed values.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2884240 ; https://softmath.seas.harvard.edu/publication/power-limited-contraction-dynamics-of-vorticella-convallaria-an-ultrafast-biological-spring/

### 49. Spore taxi service
- **Category:** Movement and travel
- **Organism:** *Paenibacillus vortex* (bacterium) carrying *Aspergillus fumigatus* spores
- **What it does:** Fungal spores cannot move on their own. This swarming bacterium picks them up and carries them for tens of centimetres across a surface. In return, the fungus's threads act as bridges that let the bacteria cross gaps they could not otherwise swim over.
- **How it works:** Swarming *P. vortex* binds conidia through specific flagella-to-surface-protein contacts and transports them within the swarm. The interaction is specific: transport was inhibited by proteolytic treatment of the conidia or by adding purified *P. vortex* flagella.
- **Numbers:** Conidia were carried at least 30 cm at rates up to about 10.8 mm per hour. Conidia were rescued and transported from niches of adverse growth conditions, and carried into areas where antibiotics inhibited bacterial growth, so the fungus escaped bacterial competition there. Related swarmers *Paenibacillus polymyxa* and *Proteus mirabilis* did not transport these conidia, and conidia of other similarly sized fungi were not carried as efficiently.
- **Energy and cost:** Swarming itself is costly (surfactants, extra flagella); carrying cargo adds drag. The bacteria gain access to fungal bridges across air gaps.
- **What triggers it:** Swarm formation on a surface and physical encounter with conidia.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Needs a moist surface for swarming; the partnership is specific to particular fungal spores.
- **Confidence:** Medium-high for the transport result (Ingham et al. 2011, *PNAS* 108:19731–19736); medium for how general or mutualistic the relationship is in soil.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3241745 ; https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/806996

## 7. Sensing and reaction

### 50. Run-and-tumble chemotaxis
- **Category:** Sensing and reaction
- **Organism:** *Escherichia coli* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** A bacterium is far too small to tell which way food lies by comparing one end of itself with the other. So it swims in a straight line for about a second, checks whether things got better, and if they did it keeps going; if not, it tumbles and picks a new random direction. Over time this biased random walk carries it up the gradient.
- **How it works:** Chemoreceptors form trimers of dimers, which assemble into large cooperative signalling clusters that amplify small changes in occupancy. Receptor activity controls the kinase CheA, which phosphorylates CheY; CheY-P binds FliM in the flagellar motor and raises the probability of switching from counter-clockwise (runs) to clockwise (tumbles). CheR and CheB adjust receptor methylation, which implements the cell's short-term "memory" and gives precise adaptation to sustained stimuli.
- **Numbers:** A run lasts about a second, roughly ten times longer than a tumble, in a uniform environment. Adaptation timescale for wild-type cells is about 20 s. (I could not verify a nanomolar CheY-P sensitivity figure.)
- **Energy and cost:** Swimming dominates the energy cost (see entry 41); the computation itself is cheap — a handful of proteins and methyl groups. Methylation consumes S-adenosylmethionine.
- **What triggers it:** Changes in attractant or repellent concentration over the preceding seconds.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** It is a statistical strategy, not navigation: individual cells frequently go the wrong way. Rotational Brownian motion randomises heading within a few seconds, which sets the useful run length. "Memory" here means a few seconds of methylation state, not recall in any richer sense.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Sources:** https://chemotaxis.biology.utah.edu/publications/PDFs/Parkinson%20et%20al.,%202015.pdf ; https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1512.02542

### 51. Built-in compass (magnetosomes)
- **Category:** Sensing and reaction
- **Organism:** *Magnetospirillum magneticum* AMB-1, *M. gryphiswaldense* (bacteria)
- **What it does:** These bacteria grow a chain of tiny magnets inside themselves and are turned by the Earth's magnetic field like a compass needle. Because the field tilts into the ground, following it takes them down to the low-oxygen mud they prefer — a one-dimensional shortcut instead of a random search.
- **How it works:** Magnetosomes are membrane-bound magnetite (or greigite) crystals, grown inside invaginations of the inner membrane under the control of the *mam* genes. The actin-like protein MamK forms cytoskeletal filaments, and MamJ is thought to attach the magnetosome vesicles to them, aligning the crystals into a chain whose magnetic moments add.
- **Numbers:** Crystals are generally 35–120 nm long (one review gives ~30–120 nm; mature crystals in *Magnetospirillum* about 45 nm), a size range that gives each a single stable magnetic domain. Deleting *mamK* reduced crystal and vesicle numbers as well as chain formation in *M. gryphiswaldense*; deleting *mamGFDC* produced crystals only 75% of wild-type size and less regular. No reliable figure verified here for typical magnetosomes per cell.
- **Energy and cost:** Iron acquisition and crystal nucleation under tight control, plus a dedicated genomic island of tens of genes and a dedicated cytoskeleton. Iron is scarce, so this is a real investment.
- **What triggers it:** Magnetosome formation is induced under low-oxygen, iron-replete conditions.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** The compass gives direction only, not position — it must still be combined with aerotaxis to find the right oxygen level ("magneto-aerotaxis"). Near the magnetic equator the field is horizontal and the trick stops working.
- **Confidence:** High for the structure and alignment; medium for species-to-species differences in MamK's necessity.
- **Sources:** https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2485401_2/component/file_3267260/Scheffel8.pdf ; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2013.00344/full

### 52. Eyespot with light-gated channels
- **Category:** Sensing and reaction
- **Organism:** *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii* (green alga)
- **What it does:** This alga has an orange spot on its side that acts as a crude eye. Light hitting it opens channels in the membrane within milliseconds, changing the beat of its two flagella so it steers towards useful light and away from damaging brightness.
- **How it works:** Channelrhodopsin-1 and -2 are directly light-gated ion channels: retinal isomerisation opens the pore. ChR1 is mainly proton-selective; ChR2 also conducts monovalent and divalent cations. Photocurrents are confined to the pigmented eyespot region, and the resulting depolarisation of the eyespot-region membrane within milliseconds modulates calcium entry into the flagella, altering their relative beating and steering the cell.
- **Numbers:** Response is on the millisecond scale. (I did not verify the number of carotenoid layers in the eyespot, so no figure is given for the reflector.)
- **Energy and cost:** Very cheap sensing — the channels are passive and use the existing gradient. The steering response costs flagellar ATP.
- **What triggers it:** Blue-green light, sampled as the cell rotates about its own axis, which gives it directional information.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Needs body rotation to get direction, so it only works while swimming. Easily saturated at high light, where the response reverses to negative phototaxis.
- **Confidence:** High (Nagel et al. 2002, 2003; the basis of optogenetics).
- **Sources:** https://zhiyuan.sjtu.edu.cn/file/course/20141029105426_Nov%2011-journal%20club-Jimmy%20Zhou-Nagel2003.pdf ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6501600

### 53. Whole-cell lens
- **Category:** Sensing and reaction
- **Organism:** *Synechocystis* sp. PCC 6803 (cyanobacterium)
- **What it does:** A spherical bacterium three thousandths of a millimetre across turns out to work like a microscopic eyeball. Light passing through the cell is focused to a bright spot on the far wall, and the cell crawls away from that spot — which means it moves towards the light source.
- **How it works:** The cell body acts as a spherical microlens, focusing a high-resolution image of the light source on the membrane opposite the source. Type IV pili are then preferentially extended away from the focused spot, moving the cell towards the source. The authors report that individual cells directly and accurately sense the position of a light source rather than responding to a spatiotemporal gradient, and that the lensing-driven intensity contrast across the cell is much larger than shading by absorption or reflection would give.
- **Numbers:** Cells are about 3 µm in diameter.
- **Energy and cost:** The optics are free — they are a consequence of being a transparent sphere. The cost is in pilus-driven motility.
- **What triggers it:** Directional light.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Needs a surface for twitching motility and a reasonably directional light source. Whether other spherical bacteria do the same is an open suggestion in the paper itself, and the downstream signal processing is not understood.
- **Confidence:** Medium. One elegant paper (Schuergers et al. 2016, *eLife* 5:e12620), widely cited but not extensively replicated.
- **Sources:** https://elifesciences.org/articles/12620 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4758948

### 54. Camera-like eye (ocelloid)
- **Category:** Sensing and reaction
- **Organism:** *Erythropsidinium*, *Warnowia*, *Nematodinium* (warnowiid dinoflagellates)
- **What it does:** A single cell contains a structure with the parts of an eye: a clear lens, a dark pigment cup and a layered retina-like body. Even stranger, those parts are built out of captured organelles — the "cornea" from mitochondria and the "retina" from the remains of a swallowed red alga.
- **How it works:** Single-organelle genomics on uncultivated warnowiid cells showed that the cornea-like layer is formed from mitochondria, while the retinal body is built from plastids derived from a secondary red-algal endosymbiont, sitting at the core of a network of peridinin-type plastids.
- **Numbers:** No reliable figures verified here (the cells are uncultivated and rarely found).
- **Energy and cost:** Assembling and maintaining a multi-organelle optical device inside one cell is a large structural investment for an organism that cannot be cultured and is rarely encountered.
- **What triggers it:** Unknown. The behavioural response the ocelloid serves has not been directly observed.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** The cells are uncultivated and rarely seen in environmental samples, which is exactly why function and origin have been hard to pin down. Some researchers suggest the retinal body may mainly support energy production rather than vision.
- **Confidence:** Medium for the structure and organelle origins (Gavelis et al. 2015, *Nature* 523:204–207); **low** for the claim that it is used for vision — that remains unproven and is actively questioned.
- **Sources:** https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/single-celled-creature-has-eye-made-of-domesticated-microbes ; https://ersearch2.cvtisr.sk/vufind/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c586t-93d81d74e4f60ea62ed89bd5220ae23a0d1b644451b3bd5f3f2cae3336c846973

### 55. All-or-nothing sugar switch
- **Category:** Sensing and reaction
- **Organism:** *Escherichia coli* (lac system)
- **What it does:** Faced with an intermediate amount of a sugar, the cells do not all switch on halfway. Instead the population splits: some cells are fully on, others fully off. Which one a cell picks depends partly on what it was doing before — the system remembers its own history.
- **How it works:** The permease LacY imports the inducer; the inducer increases LacY expression; that positive feedback loop makes the system bistable. Cells were tracked with a chromosomal GFP reporter under the lac promoter.
- **Numbers:** Hysteresis was observed when extracellular TMG concentration was varied with glucose held at zero: at low TMG cells stay uninduced, at high TMG fully induced, and in the middle the state depends on history.
- **Energy and cost:** Making the full lac enzyme set is a substantial protein cost, which is why committing fully or not at all can beat a half-measure. Being in the wrong state wastes either enzymes or food.
- **What triggers it:** Inducer concentration, modulated by glucose; and the cell's prior state.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Important caveat: the classic demonstration used the gratuitous inducer TMG, and whether the wild-type operon is bistable with natural lactose is disputed — van Hoek and Hogeweg (2006) argue from simulations that it is not, while other modelling predicts bistability with lactose. So "bacteria have memory" should be stated as "this circuit shows hysteresis under these inducer conditions".
- **Confidence:** Medium. The experiment is solid (Ozbudak et al. 2004, *Nature* 427:737–740); its generality to natural lactose is contested.
- **Sources:** https://www.rpgroup.caltech.edu/cshl_pboc_2023/assets/pdfs/ozbudak2004.pdf ; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2010.00022/pdf

### 56. Escalating escape plan
- **Category:** Sensing and reaction
- **Organism:** *Stentor roeselii* (ciliate)
- **What it does:** Annoy this trumpet-shaped ciliate with a stream of irritating particles and it does not do the same thing every time. It first bends away, then tries to flick the particles out, then scrunches down into its tube, and only as a last resort detaches and swims off. Each response is tried before the next, more costly one.
- **How it works:** Dexter et al. re-examined a 1906 observation by H. S. Jennings using the same species he had used (earlier failures to replicate had used *Stentor coeruleus*, a different, tube-less species). Faced with an irritant, *S. roeselii* first bends away and alters ciliary beating to expel particles from its oral cavity; if that fails it contracts onto its holdfast, or detaches and swims away. Each organism's choice between contracting and detaching resembled a fair coin toss.
- **Numbers:** The contract-versus-detach decision was approximately 50:50 per cell.
- **Energy and cost:** The ordering itself is the economy: the cheap responses (bending, ciliary reversal) are tried first, and the expensive one — abandoning a built mucus tube and swimming away — is held back.
- **What triggers it:** Repeated mechanical/chemical irritation, in the original work with carmine particles.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** **Report the claim carefully.** What was shown is a hierarchy of avoidance behaviours with a changing response to a repeated identical stimulus, which the authors call "sequential decision making" and distinguish from ordinary habituation and conditioning. It is not learning in the associative sense, and no memory trace has been identified. Earlier non-replication was a species mismatch.
- **Confidence:** Medium. One careful 2019 paper (Dexter et al., *Current Biology*, doi 10.1016/j.cub.2019.10.059) reviving a 1906 observation; the mechanism is unknown and the "decision-making" language is interpretive.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7017933 ; https://hms.harvard.edu/news/unexpected-depths

### 57. Electrical reverse gear (action potential)
- **Category:** Sensing and reaction
- **Organism:** *Paramecium caudatum* (ciliate)
- **What it does:** When this ciliate bumps into something, an electrical pulse sweeps over it — the same kind of calcium spike a nerve cell uses. The pulse flips the direction its cilia beat, so it reverses, turns slightly and swims off again. It is a whole reflex arc in one cell.
- **How it works:** Mechanical contact at the front depolarises the membrane. Above threshold, voltage-gated Ca2+ channels located in the ciliary membrane (L-type, CaV1-family-related) open and generate a graded calcium action potential. The Ca2+ entering acts on the mechanism controlling ciliary beat direction, causing ciliary reversal and backward swimming. A fast voltage-gated K+ conductance and a slower Ca2+-activated K+ conductance repolarise the cell; the KCa channels are probably also ciliary, since no Ca2+ spills into the cell body. Touching the rear instead hyperpolarises the cell and makes it speed forward.
- **Numbers:** The cytoplasm is nearly isopotential end to end, which is why anterior and posterior cilia must have different sensitivities to membrane potential.
- **Energy and cost:** The spike itself uses the existing ion gradients; the cost is pumping calcium back out and the ATP for ciliary beating.
- **What triggers it:** Mechanical contact, chemical repellents, temperature and other stimuli.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Graded rather than all-or-none, so it is a reflex, not a digital signal. A 2022 paper notes there is still no empirically based model of the *Paramecium* action potential, so quantitative details are incomplete.
- **Confidence:** High for the mechanism (Eckert and Naitoh's classic work); medium for quantitative modelling.
- **Sources:** https://www.eneuro.org/content/8/3/ENEURO.0018-21.2021 ; https://www.biorxiv.org/content/biorxiv/early/2022/02/17/2022.02.15.480485.full.pdf

### 58. Three-protein clock
- **Category:** Sensing and reaction
- **Organism:** *Synechococcus elongatus* (cyanobacterium)
- **What it does:** This bacterium keeps a 24-hour clock so it can prepare for sunrise before it happens. Astonishingly, the clock is not a gene circuit that needs a living cell: three purified proteins and ATP in a test tube keep ticking on a 24-hour cycle on their own.
- **How it works:** KaiC is phosphorylated and dephosphorylated in a daily cycle. KaiA promotes KaiC phosphorylation; KaiB negates KaiA activity and promotes dephosphorylation. Mixing purified KaiA, KaiB and KaiC with ATP and Mg2+ reconstitutes a circadian rhythm of KaiC phosphorylation without transcription or translation — the rhythm persists when transcription and translation are inhibited in vivo too.
- **Numbers:** Standard in-vitro reaction: 3.5 µM KaiC, 1.2 µM KaiA, 3.5 µM KaiB, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM ATP, 30 °C. Altering KaiA levels changes both the period and amplitude of the rhythm.
- **Energy and cost:** Remarkably cheap: the oscillator's ATP consumption is small, and it is often cited as one of the least energy-hungry 24-hour timers known. (I did not verify a specific ATP-per-cycle figure here.)
- **What triggers it:** Free-running; entrained by light–dark cycles through metabolic signals such as the ATP/ADP ratio and redox state.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** It is a phosphorylation oscillator, so it depends on a stable protein pool; it is diluted by very fast growth. Temperature compensation is a well-known property of circadian clocks but was not confirmed for this system in the sources consulted here.
- **Confidence:** High (Nakajima et al. 2005, *Science* 308:414–415).
- **Sources:** https://bio-protocol.org/e2140 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1863508

## 8. Chemical production

### 59. Flash when touched
- **Category:** Chemical production
- **Organism:** *Lingulodinium polyedra* (dinoflagellate; formerly *Gonyaulax polyedra*)
- **What it does:** Disturb the water and the sea lights up blue. Each of these single cells carries little light-bomb organelles, and a mechanical jolt sets off a chemical chain that makes them flash for about a tenth of a second — bright enough to startle whatever bumped into them, or to advertise it to bigger predators.
- **How it works:** Scintillons are organelles containing luciferin, luciferase and a luciferin-binding protein (LBP). LBP binds luciferin at neutral-to-alkaline pH, protecting it from autoxidation, and releases it by a conformational change below pH 7 — the same pH at which luciferase becomes active. A voltage-gated proton channel (LpHV1) in the vacuole membrane feeds protons into the scintillon to produce that acid pulse; zinc inhibits both the channel current and the acid-induced flash in isolated scintillons. Upstream, mechanical stimulation is transduced through a G-protein-coupled pathway to the vacuole's electrical response.
- **Numbers:** Flash duration about 100 ms in vivo — a figure I could only source to a patent document, so treat it as approximate. LBP binds luciferin at pH 8.0 but not pH 6.3.
- **Energy and cost:** Luciferin must be resynthesised, and the cell maintains a circadian store of the machinery that is built up each night and degraded each day — a large recurring protein cost for an output used rarely.
- **What triggers it:** Mechanical shear (waves, a swimming predator), gated by the circadian clock so it only works at night.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Exhausts after repeated stimulation; useless in daylight; the cell cannot control who sees the flash.
- **Confidence:** High for the pH-switch mechanism and the proton channel; medium for the 100 ms figure.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC547824 ; https://www.chemistryworld.com/features/unravelling-the-chemistry-behind-the-seas-bioluminescent-sparkle/4022961.article

### 60. Steady glow chemistry (bacterial luciferase)
- **Category:** Chemical production
- **Organism:** *Aliivibrio fischeri* (bacterium; formerly *Vibrio fischeri*)
- **What it does:** Unlike the dinoflagellate's flash, this bacterium produces a continuous blue-green glow. It only bothers when there are enough of its own kind around to make the light visible, which is why the trick is bound up with chemical head-counting.
- **How it works:** The *lux* operon encodes luciferase (LuxAB), which oxidises reduced flavin mononucleotide (FMNH2) and a long-chain aldehyde with O2, emitting a photon; LuxCDE regenerates the aldehyde. Expression is controlled by LuxI, which synthesises the autoinducer 3-oxo-C6-homoserine lactone, and LuxR, the transcription factor that binds it and activates the operon. A second system, AinS producing C8-HSL, shares the LuxR receptor.
- **Numbers:** The LuxI-based signal is the major factor triggering and maintaining the symbiotic bottleneck in host tissue morphology.
- **Energy and cost:** Expensive: each photon costs a reduced flavin and an aldehyde, diverting reducing power from growth. This is precisely why it is held back until a quorum makes it worth doing.
- **What triggers it:** Accumulated autoinducer, which only reaches threshold at high cell density or in a confined space.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Pointless and costly for an isolated cell; mutants that stop glowing save energy and can spread as cheats, which is part of why hosts police their symbionts.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Sources:** https://microbewiki.kenyon.edu/index.php/Role_of_the_Lux_Operon_in_Bioluminescence ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10959291

### 61. Antibiotic factory
- **Category:** Chemical production
- **Organism:** *Streptomyces griseus* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** Soil bacteria wage chemical warfare on each other. This one makes streptomycin, a complex sugar-based molecule that jams the protein-making machinery of its competitors. It was the first drug that worked against tuberculosis.
- **How it works:** Streptomycin is an aminoglycoside made by a long biosynthetic pathway encoded in a gene cluster; it binds the bacterial 30S ribosomal subunit, causing misreading of mRNA and blocking translation in susceptible bacteria. The producer protects itself with a dedicated resistance mechanism in the same cluster.
- **Numbers:** Isolated in 1944 and published as Schatz, Bugie and Waksman, *Proc. Soc. Exptl. Biol. Med.* 55:66–69. (Historical note: the credit between Schatz, Bugie and Waksman was litigated in 1950 and remains contested; Waksman alone received the 1952 Nobel Prize.)
- **Energy and cost:** Secondary metabolite pathways are among the largest gene clusters bacteria carry, built and run only when growth has stopped — the cell spends its reserves on chemistry instead of offspring.
- **What triggers it:** Nutrient limitation and the onset of sporulation; production is tied to developmental transitions.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Resistance arises readily by ribosomal mutation; the producer must carry its own immunity; and the compounds are costly enough that they are made only in stationary phase.
- **Confidence:** High for the discovery and mechanism; the attribution history is the contested part, not the biology.
- **Sources:** https://waksmanmuseum.rutgers.edu ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3911012

### 62. Purple anti-grazer poison
- **Category:** Chemical production
- **Organism:** *Chromobacterium violaceum* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** This bacterium turns bright purple when there are enough of its own kind around. The purple pigment is a poison aimed not at other bacteria but at the single-celled predators that eat bacteria — it makes them kill themselves.
- **How it works:** Violacein is an alkaloid whose synthesis is regulated by an N-acylhomoserine-lactone quorum-sensing system. Purified violacein from cell extracts was highly toxic to bacterivorous nanoflagellates, and antiprotozoal activity correlated positively with strain violacein content. In the later biofilm study, nanomolar violacein inhibited protozoan feeding by inducing a conserved eukaryotic cell-death program, and the pigment is produced predominantly within biofilm cells.
- **Numbers:** Active at nanomolar concentrations against protozoa. In the 2008 survey, 17 marine bacterial strains released grazer-killing compounds when they formed biofilms.
- **Energy and cost:** A complex secondary metabolite, and a public good — any neighbour in the biofilm benefits. Quorum control means it is only made when the group is large enough to share the cost.
- **What triggers it:** Quorum sensing; biofilm formation strongly increases production.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Does not stop a predator that does not eat the cells (it works at the ingestion stage), and the bright pigment is a conspicuous signal. Cheats that do not make violacein benefit for free.
- **Confidence:** Medium-high. Two separate studies — the 2004 *AEM* paper (planktonic cells, quorum regulation) and the 2008 *PLoS ONE* paper (biofilms, cell-death mechanism) — are often conflated; both support the core claim.
- **Sources:** https://www.io-warnemuende.de/files/bio/ag-molbio/pdf/AEM%2070%201593.pdf ; https://plos.altmetric.com/details/doi/10.1371/journal.pone.0002744

### 63. Frost maker
- **Category:** Chemical production
- **Organism:** *Pseudomonas syringae* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** Pure water can stay liquid well below 0 °C. This bacterium makes a protein that gives water molecules a template to line up on, so ice forms at temperatures as warm as about −2 °C. It does this to crack open plant tissue and get at the nutrients inside — and the same protein is used industrially to make artificial snow.
- **How it works:** The ice-nucleation protein InaZ sits in the outer membrane and presents arrays of repeat units that act as ice-nucleation active sites, ordering interfacial water into an ice-like lattice. Nucleation happens at the interface between single layers of protein interacting with only a few layers of water molecules. The protein becomes more active as temperature falls, reorienting at lower temperatures in a way that enhances order-inducing water interactions.
- **Numbers:** With *P. syringae* present, water can freeze at temperatures as high as about −2 °C.
- **Energy and cost:** One large repetitive surface protein, expressed in a minority of cells; the payoff is frost damage to plants that releases nutrients the bacterium can use.
- **What triggers it:** Low temperature activates the protein itself; expression is regulated by growth conditions.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Only helps when it is cold enough to be near freezing anyway. The fundamental mechanism of protein-driven ice nucleation is still not fully clear.
- **Confidence:** High for the phenomenon and the −2 °C threshold; medium for the molecular mechanism, which is actively researched.
- **Sources:** https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7895962/ ; https://www.the-scientist.com/microbial-ice-makers-33659

### 64. Cellulose raft
- **Category:** Chemical production
- **Organism:** *Komagataeibacter xylinus* (bacterium; the kombucha/nata de coco organism)
- **What it does:** This bacterium extrudes ribbons of pure cellulose — the same material plants use — out of its own side. The ribbons tangle together into a thick floating mat that holds the whole colony at the surface of the liquid, where the oxygen is.
- **How it works:** The cellulose synthase complex BcsA/BcsB polymerises UDP-glucose into glucan chains and extrudes them through the membrane. Protofibrils assemble into ribbon-shaped microfibrils that stay attached to the cell envelope even during division; cryo-electron tomography shows crystallisation occurring serially after secretion along one side of the cell, producing a ribbon that can reach several micrometres, apparently guided by an uncharacterised cytoskeleton. Mats form at the liquid–air interface, where higher oxygen concentration supports aerobic growth.
- **Numbers:** Ribbons can reach several micrometres in length. No reliable figure verified here for extrusion rate or energetic cost per glucose.
- **Energy and cost:** Every glucose spent on cellulose is glucose not spent on growth, and the polymer is never recovered — a pure infrastructure investment in staying near oxygen.
- **What triggers it:** Static culture at a liquid–air interface; production is constitutive in the right conditions.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** The mat traps the colony: cells deep inside it are oxygen-starved. The sources consulted describe the pellicle as a consequence of interface growth and do not firmly establish its evolutionary purpose, so the "raft" reading is an inference.
- **Confidence:** High for the chemistry and structure; medium for the functional interpretation.
- **Sources:** https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/dw7sg-enw51/preview/Journal_of_Bacteriology-2021-Nicolas-e00371-20.full.pdf ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6740014

### 65. Sea-smell molecule (DMSP)
- **Category:** Chemical production
- **Organism:** *Emiliania huxleyi* (coccolithophore); also *Phaeocystis* spp.
- **What it does:** These algae make huge amounts of a sulfur compound that does several jobs at once inside the cell. When the cell is eaten or dies, the compound breaks down into a gas that smells of the sea, drifts into the air, and may help seed clouds.
- **How it works:** Dimethylsulfoniopropionate (DMSP) serves as an osmolyte, an antioxidant, a predator deterrent and a cryoprotectant; it is cleaved enzymatically to dimethyl sulfide (DMS) plus acrylate. DMS escaping to the atmosphere is oxidised to sulfate aerosols that can act as cloud condensation nuclei — the basis of the CLAW hypothesis.
- **Numbers:** No reliable per-cell figures verified here. *E. huxleyi* and *Phaeocystis* blooms covering extensive areas are the best-studied prolific producers.
- **Energy and cost:** DMSP is carbon- and sulfur-expensive to accumulate to molar-scale internal concentrations, which is why its multiple roles matter — it earns its keep several ways at once.
- **What triggers it:** Osmotic stress, oxidative stress and grazing; baseline production is constitutive.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** **The climate link is the weak part.** Individual links in the phytoplankton–DMS–CCN–cloud albedo loop have been affirmed, but there has been no overall synthesis capable of adequately testing the hypothesis at a global scale, and there is still no general consensus on the global validity of the original CLAW hypothesis. The cellular roles are much better supported than the climate feedback.
- **Confidence:** High for DMSP's cellular roles and DMS production; **low** for the CLAW climate feedback.
- **Sources:** https://www.publish.csiro.au/en/fulltext/EN07080 ; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2017.00637/full

### 66. Smell of rain lures couriers
- **Category:** Chemical production
- **Organism:** *Streptomyces coelicolor* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** The earthy smell after rain comes from soil bacteria. It turns out that smell is not a by-product: the bacteria only make it when they are producing spores, and it attracts small soil animals that then carry the spores away on and inside their bodies.
- **How it works:** Geosmin and 2-methylisoborneol (2-MIB) are terpenoid volatiles whose genes are expressed only when the bacteria make spores. Springtail (Collembola) antennae respond electrophysiologically to both compounds, and springtails show clear attraction to geosmin in Y-tube assays. Springtails carry spores on their bodies and also pass live spores through their guts; in one test live *Streptomyces* spores emerged from the springtails' hindguts.
- **Numbers:** In a network of field traps baited with *S. coelicolor* colonies, there was significant attraction of springtails relative to unbaited controls.
- **Energy and cost:** Two terpenoid pathways, run only at sporulation. The bacteria are also eaten by the springtails they attract, so the cost includes being food.
- **What triggers it:** Sporulation — the genes are developmentally regulated.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** The arrangement attracts a grazer as well as a courier, so the net benefit depends on the balance. The sources consulted give no information on how the 2020 finding has been challenged or extended.
- **Confidence:** Medium-high. One strong multi-method paper (Becher et al. 2020, *Nature Microbiology* 5:821–829), not yet widely replicated.
- **Sources:** https://www.gwern.net/doc/biology/2020-becher.pdf ; https://www.globalsoilbiodiversity.org/blog-beneath-our-feet/2020/12/22/the-scent-of-earth-a-connection-between-sporulating-soil-bacteria-and-springtails

## 9. Rest, dormancy and survival of extremes

### 67. Endospore
- **Category:** Rest, dormancy and survival of extremes
- **Organism:** *Bacillus subtilis* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** Faced with starvation, this bacterium builds a tiny armoured capsule inside itself, packs a copy of its genome into it, and then dissolves away, leaving the capsule behind. The capsule has almost no water and no metabolism at all, and it can survive boiling, drying and radiation for a very long time.
- **How it works:** Asymmetric division creates a forespore engulfed by the mother cell. The core is dehydrated and loaded with Ca-dipicolinic acid (DPA); DNA is coated with α/β-type small acid-soluble proteins (SASP) that change its conformation and protect it; multiple layers of cortex peptidoglycan and coat proteins are added; the mother cell then lyses.
- **Numbers:** Ca-DPA makes up about 25% of core dry weight. DPA-less spores show lower resistance to wet heat, dry heat, hydrogen peroxide and desiccation, but similar UV resistance. Wet-heat resistance is determined largely by core water content, which is much lower than in a growing cell; higher core water correlates with lower wet-heat resistance. DPA does not appear to contribute to dry-heat resistance, where α/β-type SASP protect the DNA. (No verified figure for maximum survival time in years; claims of millions of years are contested and were not verified here.)
- **Energy and cost:** Sporulation takes hours, is irreversible once committed, and kills the mother cell. Germination then costs a full rebuild of the metabolic machinery.
- **What triggers it:** Starvation, especially carbon and nitrogen limitation, combined with high cell density.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Slow to commit and slow to wake; a spore cannot take advantage of a brief return of food. Germination can be triggered inappropriately by nutrient germinants, which is how sporicidal strategies work.
- **Confidence:** High for the structure and resistance mechanisms; low for the headline-grabbing longevity claims.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1482921 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC110995

### 68. Shattered-genome repair
- **Category:** Rest, dormancy and survival of extremes
- **Organism:** *Deinococcus radiodurans* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** A dose of radiation that smashes this bacterium's chromosome into hundreds of pieces does not kill it. Over a few hours it finds the overlapping ends of the fragments, stitches them back together in the right order, and carries on as if nothing had happened.
- **How it works:** Repair proceeds in two phases. First, extended synthesis-dependent strand annealing (ESDSA): fragments are resected, single strands invade overlapping fragments, and DNA polymerase I extends them to make long linear intermediates which anneal into whole chromosomes. Then RecA-mediated homologous recombination finishes the job. ESDSA depends on RecA. Separately, the cell maintains an exceptional pool of manganese antioxidants — a ternary complex of manganese with phosphate and a small peptide — which protects proteins (especially the repair enzymes) from oxidation, so the repair machinery still works after the dose.
- **Numbers:** D10 (dose for 90% kill) is about 12.5–12.7 kGy, against under 1 kGy for *E. coli* and *B. subtilis*. The cell keeps up to 10 genome copies in exponential growth, with at least 2 always available as recombination templates. Reported double-strand-break burdens vary widely by assumption — one estimate gives about 80 DSBs per haploid genome at 5,000 Gy, while other sources describe hundreds of breaks and thousands of single-strand breaks at high dose. The cell completely reassembles its shattered chromosome from hundreds of short fragments in just a few hours. Dried and frozen cells have been reported to survive 140,000 Gy.
- **Energy and cost:** Massive DNA synthesis in a few hours, plus the standing cost of multiple genome copies and a large manganese-antioxidant pool.
- **What triggers it:** DNA damage from radiation, desiccation or oxidative stress. (Radiation resistance is widely interpreted as a side-effect of adaptation to desiccation, which causes similar damage.)
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Needs at least two intact copies' worth of overlapping information — damage beyond that is unrecoverable. It does not protect against damage during the dose, only repair afterwards.
- **Confidence:** High for ESDSA and the manganese-antioxidant role (Zahradka et al. 2006, *Nature*; Daly lab work including a 2024 *PNAS* paper); medium for any specific DSB count, which varies by source.
- **Sources:** https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2797637/ ; https://news.northwestern.edu/stories/2024/12/how-conan-the-bacterium-withstands-extreme-radiation

### 69. Life at 122 °C
- **Category:** Rest, dormancy and survival of extremes
- **Organism:** *Methanopyrus kandleri* strain 116 (archaeon)
- **What it does:** This archaeon from a deep-sea hydrothermal vent holds the record for the hottest temperature at which anything is known to grow and reproduce — hotter than a domestic pressure cooker. The pressure of the deep sea is part of what makes it possible.
- **How it works:** High hydrostatic pressure raises the maximum temperature for cell proliferation, apparently by stabilising biomolecules against thermal denaturation. The organism is a hydrogenotrophic methanogen, reducing CO2 with H2 to make methane.
- **Numbers:** Maximum growth temperature rises from 116 °C at 0.4 MPa to 122 °C at 20 MPa. The strain was isolated from the Kairei hydrothermal field on the Central Indian Ridge. Cells survived 130 °C for up to 2 hours at normal pressure and over 3 hours under high pressure. The carbon kinetic isotope effect at 122 °C and 40 MPa was reported as −9.4‰, among the smallest ever reported (a conference abstract by the same author gives different values, so treat the isotope number cautiously). Takai et al. 2008, *PNAS* 105:10949–10954.
- **Energy and cost:** Everything — proteins, membranes, nucleic acids — must be built for stability, which generally costs flexibility and catalytic speed. Growth at the limit is slow.
- **What triggers it:** Not a switch; this is the organism's growth envelope.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Needs high pressure to reach the record; at atmospheric pressure the ceiling is 116 °C. It is an obligate anaerobe and is killed by oxygen.
- **Confidence:** High for the growth record.
- **Sources:** https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008PNAS..10510949T/abstract ; https://www.jamstec.go.jp/e/about/press_release/20080729/index.html

### 70. Life in acid at pH 0
- **Category:** Rest, dormancy and survival of extremes
- **Organism:** *Picrophilus torridus*, *P. oshimae* (archaea)
- **What it does:** These archaea grow best in acid roughly as strong as battery acid, and can still grow at around pH 0. Most acid-lovers keep their insides near neutral; this one does not even do that — its cytoplasm is acidic too.
- **How it works:** The cell maintains an unusually low internal pH of about 4.6, in contrast to other thermoacidophiles which keep internal pH near neutral — so its cytoplasmic enzymes must themselves be acid-adapted, and the membrane need only hold a much smaller pH gradient. The membrane is built for extremely low proton permeability.
- **Numbers:** pH optimum 0.7; growth occurs even around pH 0; growth range about pH 0–3.5 and 45–65 °C with an optimum of 60 °C. Intracellular pH about 4.6 (Schleper et al. 1995).
- **Energy and cost:** Holding any pH gradient against a 10,000-fold proton difference costs continuous proton pumping; the unusual solution of letting the cytoplasm go acidic reduces that bill but requires a whole acid-stable proteome.
- **What triggers it:** Not a switch; this is the growth envelope.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** So specialised that it cannot grow at pH values above 4.0 — it is trapped in its niche.
- **Confidence:** High for the growth range and internal pH; one secondary source attributes the 1995 paper to a different first author, so check the citation before quoting it.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC177581 ; https://microbewiki.kenyon.edu/index.php/Picrophilus

### 71. Salt-in survival
- **Category:** Rest, dormancy and survival of extremes
- **Organism:** *Halobacterium salinarum* (archaeon)
- **What it does:** Living in brine that would shrivel almost anything, this archaeon does not fight the salt — it matches it, filling itself with potassium chloride until its insides are as salty as its outside. Its proteins are rebuilt to work only in that brine.
- **How it works:** The "salt-in" strategy: molar concentrations of K+ and Cl- are accumulated in the cytoplasm, balancing the external osmotic pressure. This forces a proteome that is highly acidic — negatively charged surfaces that stay hydrated and soluble at high ionic strength. Most such proteins denature in low salt.
- **Numbers:** No specific molarity verified here beyond "molar levels". The strategy occurs in the aerobic Halobacteriaceae (archaea), *Salinibacter* (bacteria) and the anaerobic Halanaerobiales (bacteria).
- **Energy and cost:** KCl is a bioenergetically inexpensive osmoprotectant compared with synthesising organic compatible solutes such as glycine betaine — once the acidic proteome has evolved. The cost is the irreversibility: the cell must permanently maintain high cytoplasmic KCl.
- **What triggers it:** Not a switch; it is constitutive.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Lyses in fresh water. The strategy is rare precisely because the whole proteome must be committed to it. One exception complicates the picture: *Halorhodospira halophila* retains an acidic proteome at seawater salinity without accumulating cytoplasmic KCl beyond *E. coli* levels.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Sources:** https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2013.00315/full ; https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41598-020-59231-9

### 72. Armoured cyst
- **Category:** Rest, dormancy and survival of extremes
- **Organism:** *Acanthamoeba castellanii* (amoeba)
- **What it does:** When conditions turn bad, this amoeba rounds up and builds a two-layered wall around itself, partly out of cellulose — the same stuff as plant cell walls. The cyst shrugs off disinfectants, drying and heat, and can wait for years before cracking open again.
- **How it works:** The cyst wall is double-layered, with an inner endocyst and outer ectocyst, mainly composed of cellulose and proteins. Resistance develops as the wall is built: in synchronous encystment, resistance to chemical agents and to moist heat developed between 14 and 24 h after inoculation into encystment medium, and cellulose was detected at around 16 h. The authors concluded that resistance probably results largely from the physical barrier of the cyst walls rather than from metabolic dormancy as such.
- **Numbers:** Cellulose makes up up to 35% of the cyst wall in *A. castellanii*. Cysts have been reported to survive desiccation for more than 20 years (a single-source claim).
- **Energy and cost:** Hours of synthesis to build the wall and a complete shutdown of feeding and division; excystment then costs a rebuild.
- **What triggers it:** Starvation, desiccation, osmotic stress and adverse chemistry.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Takes the better part of a day to become resistant, so it is no help against a sudden insult. Resistance to disinfectants makes *Acanthamoeba* a persistent problem in contact-lens solutions and water systems — the sources consulted note chlorine resistance as an emerging concern but give no cyst-specific chlorine dose data.
- **Confidence:** High for structure and the timing of resistance; medium for the 20-year survival figure.
- **Sources:** https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11945136/ ; https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/128040/

### 73. Growth at −15 °C
- **Category:** Rest, dormancy and survival of extremes
- **Organism:** *Planococcus halocryophilus* Or1 (bacterium)
- **What it does:** This bacterium from Arctic permafrost does not merely survive freezing — it actually grows and divides at −15 °C, in salty brine veins that stay liquid below zero. It is still metabolically active at −25 °C.
- **How it works:** Survival and growth at these temperatures depend on remodelling the cytoplasmic membrane towards a higher ratio of saturated to branched fatty acids, unusual encrustations surrounding the cells, and multi-copy osmolyte-uptake genes that give redundancy against combined cold and osmotic stress. The brine that remains liquid at subzero temperatures is what makes life possible at all.
- **Numbers:** Growth and division at −15 °C, the lowest demonstrated at the time (previous record −12 °C); metabolic activity at −25 °C in frozen permafrost microcosms. Culture medium was tryptic soy broth with 18% NaCl, which stayed liquid at −15 °C and froze inconsistently between −16 and −18 °C; cultures were held at −15 °C for 130 days. Genome 3.4 Mbp. Isolated from active-layer soil at Eureka, Ellesmere Island.
- **Energy and cost:** Growth at −15 °C is extraordinarily slow and must be paid for alongside coping with 18% salt — cold and osmotic stress at once.
- **What triggers it:** Not a switch; it is the growth envelope.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Requires high salt to keep water liquid, so cold tolerance and salt tolerance are inseparable here. Doubling times at the limit are on the order of months.
- **Confidence:** Medium-high. One well-cited study (Mykytczuk et al. 2013, *ISME J* 7:1211–1226); records of this kind are regularly revised.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3660685 ; https://www.nature.com/articles/ismej20138

### 74. 100-million-year standby
- **Category:** Rest, dormancy and survival of extremes
- **Organism:** Aerobic bacteria from oxic subseafloor sediment (mixed communities, South Pacific Gyre)
- **What it does:** Mud laid down when dinosaurs were alive was drilled up from under the Pacific. When researchers fed the microbes in it, most of them woke up and started dividing. They appear to have been alive, barely ticking over, for a hundred million years.
- **How it works:** The sediment is oxic and extremely nutrient-poor, so cells persist at vanishingly low metabolic rates. Incubation with labelled substrates showed uptake and growth, demonstrating that the cells were viable rather than merely preserved as DNA.
- **Numbers:** Up to 99.1% of the microbes in sediment deposited 101.5 million years ago were revivable. Samples came from IODP Expedition 329 (collected 2010). Anaerobic microbes were only minimally revived from this oxic sediment. Morono et al. 2020, *Nature Communications* 11:3626.
- **Energy and cost:** The energy flux available is extraordinarily small; how the cells repaired damage over that span on so little energy is explicitly unresolved by the authors.
- **What triggers it:** Return of nutrients.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** These are not spores — the mechanism of persistence is unknown, and 100-million-year survival of non-dormant cells is a strong claim resting largely on one paper and on contamination controls. Slow in-situ turnover with occasional division cannot be fully excluded.
- **Confidence:** Medium. Carefully done and widely reported, but a single study of an extraordinary claim.
- **Sources:** https://doaj.org/article/60168f65ac9e4caf9cbce09bff711b44 ; https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/07/200728113533.htm

### 75. Century-old seed bank
- **Category:** Rest, dormancy and survival of extremes
- **Organism:** *Skeletonema marinoi* (diatom)
- **What it does:** Diatoms drop resting cells into the mud, which build up in layers like tree rings. Researchers can drill a core, date each layer, and bring diatoms back to life from mud laid down a hundred years ago — then compare them with their own descendants living today.
- **How it works:** Resting stages settle into anoxic sediment, where low temperature, darkness and anoxia preserve viability; germination resumes when they are returned to light and nutrients. Dating is by ²¹⁰Pb.
- **Numbers:** Strains were revived from anoxic layers of a ²¹⁰Pb-dated sediment core accumulated over more than 100 years, corresponding to over 40,000 diatom mitotic generations, from Mariager Fjord, Denmark. The population's genetic structure was essentially stable over that century, though strains from different periods differed in some respects. (One review table lists the 22 cm layer as >80 years old rather than >100, so the chronology differs between sources.) Härnström et al. 2011, *PNAS* 108:4252–4257.
- **Energy and cost:** A resting stage is cheap to make and cheap to hold, but it is out of the game — the sediment bank is a hedge, not a growth strategy.
- **What triggers it:** Nutrient depletion and darkness trigger resting-stage formation; resuspension into light and nutrients triggers germination.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Only viable where the sediment stays cold and anoxic; germination rates fall with depth and age.
- **Confidence:** High for the revival and the genetic-stability result; medium for the exact layer ages.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3054006 ; https://gup.ub.gu.se/file/110529

## 10. Cooperation, communication and social behaviour

### 76. Count heads, then glow (quorum sensing)
- **Category:** Cooperation, communication and social behaviour
- **Organism:** *Aliivibrio fischeri* (bacterium) in the light organ of the bobtail squid *Euprymna scolopes*
- **What it does:** Each bacterium leaks a small molecule into its surroundings and also listens for it. In open water the molecule washes away and nothing happens; packed into a squid's light organ, it builds up until every cell hears it and they all switch on their lights together. The squid uses the glow to erase its own shadow in moonlight — and every dawn it throws most of the bacteria out and lets the survivors regrow.
- **How it works:** LuxI synthesises the autoinducer 3-oxo-C6-homoserine lactone, which diffuses freely across the inner membrane. LuxR binds it and activates transcription of the *lux* operon, including *luxI* itself, giving positive feedback and a sharp threshold. A second system, AinS producing C8-HSL, feeds into the same LuxR receptor.
- **Numbers:** The squid vents about 90% of the symbiont population each dawn (one widely repeated figure says up to 95%; 90% is the better-sourced number and appears in the original 1998 study). The LuxI-based signal is the major factor triggering and maintaining the host-tissue bottleneck.
- **Energy and cost:** Making the signal is cheap; acting on it is not (see entry 60). The system exists precisely to avoid paying the expensive cost at useless densities.
- **What triggers it:** Accumulated autoinducer, which reports both population density and confinement.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** The signal reports diffusion as much as density, so a single cell in a tight space can "quorum" alone. Cheats that listen but do not glow can invade; the host's daily venting plus selection on the light organ is part of how the partnership stays honest.
- **Confidence:** High. This is the system in which quorum sensing was discovered.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10959291 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euprymna_scolopes

### 77. Aggregate and build a stalk
- **Category:** Cooperation, communication and social behaviour
- **Organism:** *Dictyostelium discoideum* (social amoeba)
- **What it does:** When the food runs out, tens of thousands of separate amoebae stream together into one slug-like body, crawl to the surface, and build a tower. Some of the cells become the dead stalk holding the tower up; the rest become spores on top and get carried away. The stalk cells die so the spore cells can travel.
- **How it works:** Starving cells secrete and relay cAMP, producing outward-propagating waves that drive chemotactic streaming into aggregates. The aggregate forms a migrating slug with an anterior prestalk and posterior prespore region, then culminates: prestalk cells vacuolate, build cellulose walls and die, pushing the prespore mass upward into a sorus.
- **Numbers:** Aggregation waves have a periodicity of the order of 5–10 minutes. The species self-organises across initial cell densities of 10^4–10^6 cells/cm². (The commonly quoted figures of "100,000 cells" per fruiting body and "20% of cells die as stalk" were not verified in the sources consulted here, so treat them as approximate folklore rather than measured constants.)
- **Energy and cost:** Stalk cells pay with their lives. Because aggregates in nature can be chimeric — made of several genotypes — this is a genuine altruism problem, and cheater strains that preferentially become spores exist.
- **What triggers it:** Starvation.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Chimeric aggregates invite cheating; kin discrimination and lottery-like allocation partly counter it. The whole process takes about a day, so it is a last resort.
- **Confidence:** High for the mechanism; medium for the specific numbers often quoted.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3456464 ; https://www.on.kitp.ucsb.edu/online/multicell-c13/iwasa/pdf/Iwasa_Multicell13Conf_KITP.pdf

### 78. Specialist nitrogen cells
- **Category:** Cooperation, communication and social behaviour
- **Organism:** *Anabaena* and *Nostoc* spp. (filamentous cyanobacteria)
- **What it does:** These cyanobacteria grow as chains of cells. When nitrogen runs short, roughly every tenth cell along the chain gives up photosynthesis and reproduction forever, thickens its wall, and becomes a dedicated nitrogen-fixing factory that feeds its neighbours. The chain decides the spacing by having each new specialist tell its neighbours not to become one.
- **How it works:** HetR is a positively autoregulated DNA-binding activator of differentiation. The diffusible inhibitors PatS (through its C-terminal RGSGR pentapeptide, which binds HetR directly) and HetN implement lateral inhibition: PatS creates the de-novo pattern from undifferentiated cells and HetN maintains it as the filament grows. A gradient of PatS originating from differentiating cells appears to be required for a normal pattern. The mature heterocyst has a thick glycolipid and polysaccharide envelope that excludes oxygen, protecting nitrogenase.
- **Numbers:** Deleting *patS* produces a multiple-contiguous-heterocyst phenotype by 24 h post-induction, which gradually resolves towards wild-type spacing. (The frequently quoted "one heterocyst every 10–15 cells" was not verified in the sources consulted here.)
- **Energy and cost:** Heterocysts are terminally differentiated: they will never divide again and cannot fix carbon, so the filament gives up roughly a tenth of its reproductive capacity permanently in exchange for nitrogen.
- **What triggers it:** Deprivation of combined nitrogen.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Irreversible — if nitrogen returns, the investment is sunk. Because heterocysts cannot photosynthesise, they depend entirely on the vegetative cells for carbon.
- **Confidence:** High for the HetR/PatS/HetN circuit and terminal differentiation; the spacing figure is medium.
- **Sources:** https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4649674/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2785261

### 79. Shared iron grabbers and cheats
- **Category:** Cooperation, communication and social behaviour
- **Organism:** *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** Iron is almost impossible to get hold of, so these bacteria secrete molecules that grab it and bring it back. The molecules float free, so any neighbour can use one — including cells that make none themselves. Those freeloaders grow faster, and if there are too many of them, everyone starves.
- **How it works:** Pyoverdine is a secreted siderophore that chelates Fe(III) with extremely high affinity and is re-imported through a specific receptor. It is a classic public good: production is costly, uptake is not restricted to the producer. Griffin, West and Buckling showed experimentally that relatedness determines how far non-producing cheats spread, and that siderophore deficiency decreases iron-limited growth in monoculture.
- **Numbers:** No reliable quantitative figure verified here for production cost or cheat invasion rate.
- **Energy and cost:** Pyoverdine synthesis is a multi-enzyme non-ribosomal peptide pathway — one of the larger metabolic investments the cell makes — and the product is given away.
- **What triggers it:** Iron limitation, via the Fur regulator and the pyoverdine sigma factor.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** The cooperation is only evolutionarily stable at high relatedness or with spatial structure that keeps the benefit near the producer. This result is a cornerstone of social evolution theory and also a proposed route to "anti-virulence" therapy.
- **Confidence:** High (Griffin, West and Buckling 2004, *Nature* 430:1024–1027, repeatedly replicated).
- **Sources:** https://research-portal.st-andrews.ac.uk/en/publications/siderophore-mediated-cooperation-and-virulence-in-pseudomonas-aer/ ; https://bmcbiol.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/1741-7007-4-21

### 80. Electrical signalling in biofilms
- **Category:** Cooperation, communication and social behaviour
- **Organism:** *Bacillus subtilis* (bacterium) in biofilms
- **What it does:** In a crowded biofilm, the cells on the outside eat all the food before it reaches the middle. The starving cells in the middle send out a wave of potassium ions that travels across the whole community, telling the outer cells to slow their eating so nutrients get through. The colony's growth then pulses.
- **How it works:** Metabolically stressed interior cells — lacking access to glutamate — release intracellular potassium through the potassium channel YugO. The released K+ alters the membrane potential of neighbouring cells, which release potassium in turn, propagating a wave. Membrane-potential oscillations matched the biofilm's growth oscillations, and deleting the potassium channel abolished the response.
- **Numbers:** No reliable figure verified here for wave speed or amplitude.
- **Energy and cost:** Signalling uses existing ion gradients rather than synthesised molecules, which is cheap; the cost is that the periphery accepts slower growth for the sake of the interior.
- **What triggers it:** Glutamate limitation in the biofilm interior.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Caution on framing: the paper's headline comparison to neurons is an analogy about ion-channel-mediated long-range signalling, not a claim that bacteria think. The result comes from one group and has attracted methodological discussion.
- **Confidence:** Medium. Prindle et al. 2015, *Nature* 527:59–63, is influential but the generality of electrical signalling in biofilms is still being established.
- **Sources:** https://prindle.northwestern.edu/files/2024/01/Prindle15.pdf ; https://prindle.northwestern.edu/files/2024/01/nrmicro3588.pdf

### 81. Bacteria-triggered rosettes
- **Category:** Cooperation, communication and social behaviour
- **Organism:** *Salpingoeca rosetta* (choanoflagellate), responding to *Algoriphagus machipongonensis* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** Choanoflagellates are the closest living single-celled relatives of animals. One of them can switch from living alone to forming a rosette of cells stuck together — and what flips the switch is a molecule made by a bacterium it eats. A chemical from a bacterium turns a single cell into a multicellular body.
- **How it works:** *Algoriphagus* produces a sulfonolipid, rosette-inducing factor 1 (RIF-1), that induces rosette development; rosettes form by incomplete cytokinesis rather than aggregation. Later work showed that *Algoriphagus* makes three structurally different lipid classes that together activate, enhance and inhibit rosette development: lysophosphatidylethanolamines have no effect alone but with the sulfonolipid RIFs reproduce the full activity of live bacteria.
- **Numbers:** RIF-1 is active at concentrations as low as 10^-17 M. All 15 *Algoriphagus* species tested induced rosettes, as did 6 of 16 other Bacteroidetes species.
- **Energy and cost:** For the choanoflagellate, rosette formation is a developmental commitment affecting feeding and dispersal; the cue itself is free, coming from its food.
- **What triggers it:** The bacterial sulfonolipid, modulated by other bacterial lipids including antagonists such as sulfobacins and IOR-1A.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Depends on the presence of the right bacteria, and the signalling is a lipid cocktail, not a single switch — which complicates simple "bacteria caused multicellularity" stories.
- **Confidence:** High for the induction (Alegado et al. 2012, *eLife* 1:e00013) and its lipid chemistry; the evolutionary interpretation is necessarily speculative.
- **Sources:** https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3463246/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4948368

### 82. Hydrodynamic chain reaction
- **Category:** Cooperation, communication and social behaviour
- **Organism:** *Spirostomum ambiguum* (ciliate)
- **What it does:** These long ciliates contract in a few thousandths of a second, releasing a chemical that deters predators. When one cell contracts, the jolt of water it makes sets off its neighbours, and a wave of contractions races through the colony far faster than any chemical signal could travel. There is no touch, no chemistry and no electricity involved — just water.
- **How it works:** A cell shortens its long body by up to 60% within milliseconds (the earlier preprint gives 50%), with accelerations reaching forces of 14 g. The resulting flow field exerts measurable forces on neighbouring cells' membranes, which triggers their contraction in turn, producing a hydrodynamic trigger wave. The authors measured receiver sensitivity with a suction-flow device mimicking a predator's feeding current, and modelled the wave with antenna and percolation theory, revealing a phase transition requiring a critical colony density.
- **Numbers:** Up to 60% body shortening within milliseconds; accelerations up to 14 g; collective communication requires a critical colony density.
- **Energy and cost:** Contraction is a stored-elastic/calcium-driven event (as in *Vorticella*), with the ATP cost in resetting. The collective version costs nothing extra per cell — the water does the signalling for free.
- **What triggers it:** A predator's feeding current, or the flow from a neighbour's contraction.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Needs a dense colony — below the critical density the wave dies out. False alarms propagate just as fast as real ones.
- **Confidence:** Medium-high. One striking paper (Mathijssen et al. 2019, *Nature* 571:560–564); the ecological significance, including synchronised toxin release, is an inference from the physics.
- **Sources:** https://prakashlab.stanford.edu/publications/collective-intercellular-communication-through-ultra-fast-hydrodynamic-trigger-waves ; https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/428573v1.full.pdf

### 83. Self or stranger border lines
- **Category:** Cooperation, communication and social behaviour
- **Organism:** *Proteus mirabilis* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** Two colonies of this swarming bacterium growing towards each other on a plate will merge seamlessly if they are the same strain, but if they are different strains a visible line appears where they meet and neither crosses. The cells are checking identity badges.
- **How it works:** A six-gene locus named *ids* (identification of self) determines the outcome. Five of the six genes are required for recognition of the parent strain as self; three are interchangeable between strains, while *idsD* and *idsE* encode strain-specific identity determinants, and *idsB*, *idsC* and *idsF* are required for recognition but are not identity determinants. Later work showed the genes are transcribed as one operon with bistable expression.
- **Numbers:** Six genes in the *ids* locus; two of them (*idsD*, *idsE*) carry the strain-specific identity information.
- **Energy and cost:** A small dedicated operon plus, in practice, associated contact-dependent killing systems — a modest cost for excluding non-kin from a shared swarm.
- **What triggers it:** Contact between swarming cells of different strains.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Only discriminates among *P. mirabilis* strains, not among species. The classic macroscopic "Dienes line" phenomenon long predates the genetics, but the sources consulted here address the genetics rather than that term.
- **Confidence:** High (Gibbs, Urbanowski and Greenberg 2008, *Science* 321:256–259).
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2567286 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3133265

## 11. Construction and shape

### 84. Glass shell (frustule)
- **Category:** Construction and shape
- **Organism:** *Thalassiosira pseudonana* and diatoms generally
- **What it does:** Diatoms build themselves a two-part box out of glass, patterned with holes in designs so precise and so varied that they are used to test microscopes. The box is grown inside a special bag in the cell and then pushed out into place.
- **How it works:** Silicic acid enters through silicon transporters (SITs) and, being small and uncharged, also by diffusion; kinetic data suggest diffusion can dominate at environmentally relevant concentrations, while SITs matter most when external silicic acid is low. Measured intracellular silicon exceeds the solubility limit, so an unidentified organic compound is thought to bind it and prevent premature polymerisation. Silica is deposited inside the silica deposition vesicle (SDV), a lipid-bilayer-bound compartment, where silaffins — zwitterionic phosphoproteins — together with long-chain polyamines template and accelerate polymerisation. Some silaffins appear "catalytic" in vitro and others "regulatory" in vivo.
- **Numbers:** Silicic acid solutions polymerise above roughly 2 mM, which is why intracellular transport must be chaperoned. *T. pseudonana* was the first diatom to have its genome sequenced and is the model for frustule morphogenesis.
- **Energy and cost:** Silica is widely argued to be a cheaper armour per unit of protection than organic walls, but the sources consulted gave no verified energy figure — so: no reliable figure found for the metabolic cost of silicification.
- **What triggers it:** The cell cycle: valve formation is tied to division, and SIT expression is cell-cycle regulated.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Absolutely dependent on dissolved silicon, so diatom blooms end when silicate runs out. The box also causes the shrinking problem of entry 23. Armour does not stop a predator that crushes or dissolves it.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Sources:** https://frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2018.00125/full ; https://storre.stir.ac.uk/retrieve/23522a35-ed6c-4323-bc4d-ca23ddb95f50/The%20Plant%20Journal%20-%202022%20-%20Skeffington%20-%20Shedding%20light%20on%20silica%20biomineralization%20by%20comparative%20analysis%20of%20the.pdf

### 85. Chalk plates (coccoliths)
- **Category:** Construction and shape
- **Organism:** *Emiliania huxleyi* (coccolithophore)
- **What it does:** This alga grows interlocking plates of chalk inside itself, each one a precisely shaped wheel of calcite crystals, then pushes them out and tiles its whole surface with them. Blooms of it turn thousands of square kilometres of ocean milky white, visible from space, and their sunk remains made the white cliffs of Dover.
- **How it works:** Each coccolith is formed inside a Golgi-derived coccolith vesicle that acts as a dynamic mould for the growing plate. Calcite nucleates at multiple sites on an organic baseplate within the vesicle; trans-Golgi vesicle trafficking supplies the organic components. A calcium-rich intracellular compartment, where calcium is colocalised with phosphorus and probably stored as polyphosphate, appears to feed calcium to the vesicle. When complete, the vesicle fuses with the cell surface and the coccolith is exocytosed into the coccosphere.
- **Numbers:** Coccolith formation can be as fast as one per hour, which requires high calcium fluxes into the vesicle (a single-reference figure).
- **Energy and cost:** Calcification consumes energy for calcium transport and releases CO2 as a by-product of carbonate chemistry; the cell builds and discards an organic mould for every plate.
- **What triggers it:** Growth phase and nutrient status; calcification is suppressed in some strains and life-cycle stages.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Sensitive to ocean carbonate chemistry, hence intense interest in how it responds to acidification. What the plates are actually for — armour, light management, ballast — is still argued.
- **Confidence:** High for the mechanism; medium for the one-per-hour rate.
- **Sources:** https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_3002477_4/component/file_3002482/Article.pdf ; https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/09670260601159346

### 86. Sand-grain house
- **Category:** Construction and shape
- **Organism:** *Difflugia* spp. (testate amoebae)
- **What it does:** This amoeba cannot make its own shell, so it collects mineral grains and the glass shells of dead diatoms from the mud, sorts them by size and shape, and glues them together into a flask-shaped house that it lives inside and carries around.
- **How it works:** The test is agglutinated from foreign particles (xenosomes) obtained from the surrounding environment, embedded in a structured or sheet-like organic cement the amoeba secretes. Many species arrange the particles by size and shape, producing a shell morphology characteristic of the species; some favour flattish grains, others angular facets.
- **Numbers:** Packing of building units into depressions between mineral grains appears to be random in fossil material studied, with the number used depending on what is needed to stabilise the structure. Scanning electron microscopy with backscatter mapping has been used to study mineral selection directly.
- **Energy and cost:** The amoeba must gather and store a stock of grains before building, and secrete organic cement. The house is heavy and must be dragged everywhere.
- **What triggers it:** Division: a daughter needs a new test, built from the stockpile. (I could not verify the detailed account of daughter-cell shell construction in the sources consulted.)
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Depends on the right grains being available locally — the shell's composition reflects its habitat, which is why these amoebae are used as palaeoenvironmental indicators. The house slows the cell down and restricts where it can go.
- **Confidence:** High for the construction and selection behaviour; medium for the division-linked building sequence.
- **Sources:** https://arcella.nl/difflugia/ ; https://jm.copernicus.org/articles/7/233/1988/jm-7-233-1988.pdf

### 87. Strontium skeleton
- **Category:** Construction and shape
- **Organism:** Acantharia (radiolarian-group protists)
- **What it does:** Almost nothing on Earth builds with strontium sulfate — it is too soluble in seawater. These plankton do, making a skeleton of 20 needles radiating from the cell's centre, arranged by a strict geometric rule. Because the mineral dissolves after death, they vanish almost without trace.
- **How it works:** The skeleton is celestite (SrSO4). The standard body plan is 10 diametral or 20 radial spicules arranged by Müller's law (1859); clades A and B have 10 diametral spicules, clades C–F have 20 radial ones, and in clade C the spicules are joined by contractile fibrils.
- **Numbers:** 10 diametral or 20 radial spicules. Older reports of acantharians with 18 to 500 spicules are now excluded from the group as *Radiolaria incertae sedis*.
- **Energy and cost:** Concentrating strontium from seawater, where it is far rarer than calcium, is the central cost. In return the skeleton is dense and stiff for very little material.
- **What triggers it:** Developmental; the skeleton is built as the cell grows.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Celestite dissolves rapidly in seawater after death, so acantharians leave essentially no fossil record — a problem for science, but also a sign the skeleton is only stable while the living cell maintains it.
- **Confidence:** High for mineral and geometry.
- **Sources:** https://tolweb.org/Acantharia ; https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10481821

### 88. Super-glue foot
- **Category:** Construction and shape
- **Organism:** *Caulobacter crescentus* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** At the tip of its stalk this bacterium secretes a patch of adhesive a few hundred nanometres across. Per unit of area it is the strongest biological glue ever measured — strong enough that pulling the cell off a glass surface usually breaks the cell first.
- **How it works:** The holdfast is a polysaccharide adhesive secreted at the stalk tip on surface contact, binding irreversibly to a wide range of substrates including wet ones. Adhesion force was measured by micromanipulation, pulling single cells from a flexible pipette.
- **Numbers:** Adhesion of more than 68 N/mm² in the central region of contact — described by the authors as the strongest adhesion measured for a biological adhesive. Average single-cell adhesion force 0.59 ± 0.62 µN, ranging 0.11–2.26 µN. Some later sources quote 70 N/mm², and press coverage uses both figures.
- **Energy and cost:** A small amount of polysaccharide, but it is a one-way commitment: once stuck, the cell is anchored for life, which is exactly why the swarmer stage exists to choose the spot (entry 19).
- **What triggers it:** Surface contact, which accelerates holdfast synthesis within seconds.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Irreversible — there is no detaching. Press coverage noted that the researchers did not know whether it is the strongest glue on Earth overall, only the strongest measured biological one.
- **Confidence:** High for the measurement (Tsang et al. 2006, *PNAS*); the superlative is as-measured, not absolute.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1458647 ; https://www.livescience.com/4047-worldatms-strongest-glue-nature.html

### 89. Protein-shell enzyme factory
- **Category:** Construction and shape
- **Organism:** Cyanobacteria such as *Synechococcus* and *Prochlorococcus*
- **What it does:** The enzyme that fixes carbon dioxide is famously slow and easily confused by oxygen. These bacteria solve the problem by locking it inside a tiny protein shell along with a second enzyme that floods the inside with CO2 — a factory with its own atmosphere, built entirely from protein rather than membrane.
- **How it works:** The carboxysome is a polyhedral, icosahedral-geometry protein body that sequesters RuBisCO together with carbonic anhydrase. The shell is a thin protein layer whose narrow pores control the passage of small molecules; it is thought to be largely impermeable to CO2 and O2 but permeable to bicarbonate. Bicarbonate pumped into the cytosol diffuses in, and carbonic anhydrase converts it to CO2 inside, raising the local steady-state CO2 around RuBisCO, which cannot use bicarbonate directly. Two genetically distinct carboxysome types exist, differing in shell proteins and carbonic anhydrases.
- **Numbers:** The sources consulted did not confirm a diameter, so: no reliable figure found here (carboxysomes are commonly described as roughly 100–200 nm, but that was not verified).
- **Energy and cost:** The cell spends energy pumping bicarbonate to high cytosolic concentration and builds thousands of shell protein copies — the price of making a bad enzyme behave well.
- **What triggers it:** Inorganic carbon limitation increases carboxysome number and CO2-concentrating-mechanism gene expression.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Requires active bicarbonate transport, so it is only worthwhile where CO2 is scarce. Shell permeability is a delicate balance: too leaky and the CO2 escapes, too tight and substrate cannot get in.
- **Confidence:** High for the mechanism and function.
- **Sources:** https://rd.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11120-011-9656-y.pdf ; https://archive.jgi.doe.gov/publication/carboxysomal-carbonic-anhydrases-structure-and-role-in-microbial-co2-fixation

### 90. Living electric cable
- **Category:** Construction and shape
- **Organism:** Cable bacteria (*Candidatus* Electrothrix, *Ca.* Electronema; family Desulfobulbaceae)
- **What it does:** These bacteria grow in chains thousands of cells long, standing upright in seabed mud. The cells at the bottom, buried in oxygen-free sulfide, pass electrons up through the chain to the cells at the top, which are in oxygen. The whole filament works as an insulated electrical cable centimetres long — the longest biological wire known.
- **How it works:** A network of parallel conductive fibres runs through the periplasm along the whole filament, connecting the cells. Sulfide oxidation at depth releases electrons that travel up the fibres to be delivered to oxygen at the surface, spatially separating the two halves of a redox reaction across centimetres.
- **Numbers:** Periplasmic fibre conductivity up to 79 S/cm in marine cable bacteria — five orders of magnitude higher than a comparable filamentous bacterium, *T. flexile*. Conductivity follows an Arrhenius relation from −195 °C to +50 °C, indicating thermally activated transport, with room-temperature electron mobility about 0.1 cm²/Vs. Freshwater *Ca.* Electronema fibres are about 0.1 S/cm, roughly two orders of magnitude lower; nanoampere currents were measured over lengths up to 200 µm.
- **Energy and cost:** The filament must keep every cell in the chain alive and connected; breaking the cable disconnects everyone above the break from their electron acceptor.
- **What triggers it:** Steep sediment redox gradients with sulfide below and oxygen above.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Entirely dependent on an intact chain spanning the gradient. The fibres' molecular composition remains unknown; a proposal involving sulfur-ligated nickel cofactors whose conductivity drops on oxidation is preliminary.
- **Confidence:** High for long-distance electron transport and the fibre network; **medium-to-low** for the chemical identity of the conductor.
- **Sources:** https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6739318/ ; https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/91097

### 91. Square cell
- **Category:** Construction and shape
- **Organism:** *Haloquadratum walsbyi* (archaeon)
- **What it does:** Almost all cells are rods, spheres or spirals. This one is a flat square — like a postage stamp, and so thin it is nearly two-dimensional. It floats in saturated brine with gas bubbles inside it and wraps itself in a giant protein that holds a layer of water against its surface.
- **How it works:** The flat square shape, with gas vesicles at the cell borders, gives an extreme surface-to-volume ratio, which is advantageous where oxygen is scarce in dense brine. Halomucin, a very large mucin-like extracellular protein, forms a water-retaining layer that is thought to protect against desiccation. Gas vesicles provide buoyancy, keeping cells near the light.
- **Numbers:** Thickness about 0.1–0.5 µm (one source gives 0.2 µm), with sides roughly 2 µm long. Halomucin is 9,159 residues and 927 kDa — at the time of description the largest archaeal protein known; it is extracellular and only loosely attached to cells. First described in 1980; cultivation was achieved only in 2004, by two groups independently.
- **Energy and cost:** Manufacturing a near-megadalton extracellular protein and maintaining gas vesicles, in exchange for staying buoyant, hydrated and oxygenated in brine.
- **What triggers it:** Constitutive; the shape is intrinsic.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Restricted to near-saturated brine. Despite the "square bacterium" nickname, it is an archaeon. The flat shape makes it fragile and essentially non-motile.
- **Confidence:** High for shape, halomucin and gas vesicles; medium for the exact thickness.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1544339 ; https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2015.00249/full

### 92. Twisted iron ribbon
- **Category:** Construction and shape
- **Organism:** *Mariprofundus ferrooxydans* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** This bacterium makes a living by rusting iron. Rust would normally encrust and kill it, so it spins the waste out of one side of itself as a twisted ribbon, rotating as it goes, and rides away on the end of a growing corkscrew of rust.
- **How it works:** Iron oxyhydroxide is deposited on organic fibrils excreted from one side of the cell; several fibrils make up each stalk, containing iron oxyhydroxide crystals only a few nanometres across. A contemporaneous rotation of the cell was suggested to cause the helical morphology. Mineral growth within the fibrils is retarded relative to the surroundings: lepidocrocite crystals nucleating on the fibril surface are much larger (about 100 nm). As stalks age, amorphous FeOOH becomes metastable ferrihydrite, later overprinted with lepidocrocite.
- **Numbers:** Stalk excretion averaged 2.2 µm per hour during growth, observed by time-lapse light microscopy (Chan et al. 2011, *ISME J*).
- **Energy and cost:** Iron oxidation at neutral pH yields very little energy per electron, so the cell must process a great deal of iron for a small return — and must dispose of all the resulting mineral, which is what the stalk is for.
- **What triggers it:** Microaerophilic growth on Fe(II); stalk formation is tied to growth.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Needs a narrow oxygen window: too much oxygen and iron rusts abiotically, stealing the substrate. Later work on a related strain found stalks may contain much less organic carbon than the organic-matrix model assumed.
- **Confidence:** High for the stalk, its growth rate and its mineralogy; medium for the rotation explanation and the organic-matrix model.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3105749 ; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2016.00796/full

## 12. Anything else astonishing

### 93. Shortest-path network
- **Category:** Anything else astonishing
- **Organism:** *Physarum polycephalum* (plasmodial slime mould; a giant single multinucleate cell)
- **What it does:** Put food at two points of a maze and let this organism spread through it. Within hours it withdraws from the dead ends and leaves a single thick tube along the shortest route between the two food sources. Given food laid out like the cities around Tokyo, it produces a network strikingly similar to the real rail system.
- **How it works:** The plasmodium is one enormous cell with many nuclei and a network of tubes in which protoplasm shuttles back and forth. Tubes carrying more flow thicken and tubes carrying less shrink — a simple positive-feedback adaptation rule. Parts in dead ends shrink until the minimum-length tube is selected from the existing possibilities. Mathematical analysis (Bonifaci, Mehlhorn and Varma) later proved that a model of this dynamics converges to the shortest path.
- **Numbers:** In the Tokyo experiment, the resulting networks matched the real rail network's cost, transport efficiency and fault tolerance. Nakagaki, Yamada and Tóth 2000, *Nature* 407:470; Tero et al. 2010, *Science*.
- **Energy and cost:** Maintaining tubes costs protoplasm, which is exactly why unused tubes are withdrawn — the "computation" is a side-effect of a resource-economy rule, not an added expense.
- **What triggers it:** Food placed at separated points; the organism must first spread through the whole arena.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** **Be careful with the word "intelligence".** What was shown is that a simple flow-reinforcement rule running in a physical network relaxes to an efficient solution. The organism does not plan, has no representation of the maze, and must physically occupy the whole space first — it cannot solve a maze it has not grown into. The Tokyo result also depended on experimenter-chosen food placement and light-based barriers standing in for geography.
- **Confidence:** High for the experimental results; the mechanism is well modelled, the "intelligence" framing is not supported.
- **Sources:** https://www.nature.com/articles/35035159 ; https://www.informatics-europe.org/images/ECSS/ECSS2017/Slides/ECSS2017_Mehlhorn2.pdf

### 94. Cell the size of a fist
- **Category:** Anything else astonishing
- **Organism:** *Syringammina fragilissima* (xenophyophore; a giant foraminiferan)
- **What it does:** On the deep-sea floor there are organisms up to 20 cm across that appear to be single cells with thousands of nuclei. They build a sprawling crust of branching tubes out of cemented sediment, and almost nothing is known about how they feed or reproduce.
- **How it works:** A single multinucleate cell expands to form hundreds of hollow, branched and interconnecting tubes, cementing sediment particles into a crusty test. There is no cell division into separate cells — the body is one cytoplasmic mass with many nuclei.
- **Numbers:** Up to 20 cm across, described as the largest single-celled organism known. First xenophyophore described; discovered in 1882 by John Murray, described by Henry Bowman Brady in 1883.
- **Energy and cost:** Growing a body this large as one cell means transporting material across tens of centimetres of cytoplasm, which is presumably slow and is the reason such organisms are confined to quiet deep-sea settings.
- **What triggers it:** Not applicable — this is its ordinary growth form.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** It is not known how the organism feeds or reproduces; high lipid concentrations in the cytosol suggest it may feed on sediment bacteria. The sources available are largely encyclopedic, and they disagree on the collection locality.
- **Confidence:** Medium. The size and multinucleate single-cell nature are well attested, but the biology is genuinely unknown and good primary sources are scarce.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syringammina ; https://tolweb.org/Acantharia (for general giant-protist context; the primary references are Brady 1883 and Hughes & Gooday 2004)

### 95. Puppet-master parasite
- **Category:** Anything else astonishing
- **Organism:** *Toxoplasma gondii* (apicomplexan)
- **What it does:** This parasite can only complete its life cycle inside a cat. When it infects a rat, the rat stops being afraid of the smell of cats — and in some experiments is even drawn to it. A parasite in the brain appears to be editing one specific fear.
- **How it works:** Latent infection forms cysts in the brain. Vyas et al. reported that infection converted rats' and mice's aversion to feline odours into attraction, and that the behavioural effect was highly specific when other behaviours were tested. A follow-up reported that infection increases activity in limbic regions associated with sexual attraction on exposure to cat urine.
- **Numbers:** No reliable effect-size figure verified here.
- **Energy and cost:** For the parasite, the manipulation is a by-product of persistent brain cysts rather than an obvious ongoing expenditure — but it reduces host survival, which is the point.
- **What triggers it:** Chronic (latent) infection.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** **Treat the popular version with caution.** The original findings (Berdoy et al. 2000; Vyas et al. 2007) are real, but the sources consulted here did not surface either a clean independent replication or a published failure, and effects reported in the literature can depend on odour dose. Claims about *Toxoplasma* changing human personality or risk-taking are a much weaker and more contested literature and are not supported here.
- **Confidence:** Medium for the rodent result; low for any extension to humans.
- **Sources:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2430144 ; https://edisciplinas.usp.br/pluginfile.php/6775743/mod_resource/content/1/Behavioral%20changes%20induced%20by%20Toxoplasma%20infection%20of%20rodents%20are%20highly%20specific%20to%20aversion%20of%20cat%20odors.pdf

### 96. Make the host smell tasty
- **Category:** Anything else astonishing
- **Organism:** *Plasmodium falciparum* (apicomplexan; malaria)
- **What it does:** The malaria parasite needs mosquitoes to carry it onward. It releases a molecule that makes the infected person's own red blood cells give off a smell mosquitoes find more attractive — so they bite the infected host in preference to anyone else.
- **How it works:** HMBPP, a metabolite of the parasite's isoprenoid pathway, does not attract mosquitoes itself. It acts on human red blood cells, triggering them to release more carbon dioxide, aldehydes and monoterpenes; this altered volatile blend is what attracts the mosquitoes.
- **Numbers:** In a choice assay over 95% of mosquitoes chose HMBPP-spiked blood, feeding more intensively and for longer. Sporogony was increased in infected blood, and HMBPP did not reduce mosquito survival or fecundity. Emami et al. 2017, *Science*, doi 10.1126/science.aah4563.
- **Energy and cost:** HMBPP is an intermediate the parasite already makes, so the manipulation costs little beyond releasing it — one reason it is plausible as an evolved strategy rather than an artefact.
- **What triggers it:** The parasite's presence in the blood, especially at gametocyte stage.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** The core experiments used artificial membrane feeders, so field relevance was initially unclear; a 2020 field follow-up found that the HMBPP-induced odour blend did attract wild *Anopheles* indoors, which strengthens the case.
- **Confidence:** Medium-high. One strong primary paper with a supporting field follow-up.
- **Sources:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28183997/ ; https://cosmosmagazine.com/science/biology/malaria-infection-smells-irresistible-to-mosquitoes

### 97. Tiny passenger archaeon
- **Category:** Anything else astonishing
- **Organism:** *Nanoarchaeum equitans*, riding *Ignicoccus hospitalis* (archaea)
- **What it does:** One of the smallest cells known rides on the outside of a much larger archaeon at a hot vent. It cannot make its own fats and is missing most of the pathways a cell needs, so it takes them from its host. It has never been grown without it.
- **How it works:** An obligate symbiont attached to the *Ignicoccus* surface. Its genome lacks most biosynthetic pathways, including lipid synthesis, so it obtains lipids and other biomolecules from the host. Because the two cannot be physically separated, sequencing the symbiont also captured a large fraction of the host genome.
- **Numbers:** Cells about 400 nm in diameter — the smallest known archaeon. Genome about 0.49 Mbp on a single circular chromosome, average GC content 31.6% — smaller than *Mycoplasma genitalium* (0.58 Mbp, 475 genes), which previously held the record. The host *Ignicoccus hospitalis* genome is described as the smallest among independent, free-living organisms. Huber et al. 2002; Waters et al. 2003, *PNAS*.
- **Energy and cost:** It has given up almost everything a free cell needs in exchange for total dependence — the extreme end of genome streamlining.
- **What triggers it:** Not applicable; the association is obligate.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Cannot live alone, at all. Its taxonomic position is disputed: Brochier et al. (2005) argued it may be a fast-evolving euryarchaeal lineage related to Thermococcales rather than a separate phylum.
- **Confidence:** High for the biology; the phylum-level placement is contested.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanoarchaeum_equitans ; https://genome.jgi.doe.gov/portal/ign_k/ign_k.home.html

### 98. Centimetre bacterium
- **Category:** Anything else astonishing
- **Organism:** *Thiomargarita magnifica* (bacterium)
- **What it does:** Bacteria are supposed to be invisible. This one grows as a single cell up to a centimetre long — you can pick it up with tweezers. Stranger still, its DNA is not loose in the cytoplasm but packed into membrane-bound compartments, something textbooks say bacteria do not do.
- **How it works:** The filament is a single cell containing membrane-bound compartments holding DNA, named "pepins" after fruit seeds, which are metabolically active. A large central vacuole keeps the active cytoplasm in a thin peripheral layer, as in its relative *T. namibiensis* (entry 38), which helps overcome the diffusion limits on cell size.
- **Numbers:** Entire filaments up to 9.66 mm long were visualised, with the species generally described as about a centimetre; roughly twelve times longer than the previous record-holder, another *Thiomargarita*. Found in Guadeloupe mangroves (first collected 2009). Volland et al. 2022, *Science*, doi 10.1126/science.abb3634.
- **Energy and cost:** Being this large requires compartmentalising transcription and translation and maintaining a huge vacuole; it also makes the organism effectively sessile and conspicuous.
- **What triggers it:** Not applicable; this is its growth form.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** Not yet cultured. It is not settled whether the pepins drove the extreme size, or whether they occur in other bacteria — and the claims rest on one paper plus press coverage, with no independent replication in the sources consulted.
- **Confidence:** High for the size measurement; medium for the significance and generality of the pepins.
- **Sources:** https://newscenter.lbl.gov/2022/06/23/giant-bacteria-found-in-guadeloupe-mangroves-challenge-traditional-concepts/ ; https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/news/2022/june/worlds-largest-bacteria-are-visible-naked-eye.html

### 99. Shattered then reassembled genome
- **Category:** Anything else astonishing
- **Organism:** *Oxytricha trifallax* (ciliate)
- **What it does:** This ciliate keeps two genomes. The working one is chopped into thousands of tiny single-gene chromosomes. The archival one is far worse: the pieces of each gene are scrambled, out of order, sometimes reversed, and interleaved with pieces of other genes. Every time the cell has sex it must unscramble the whole thing and rebuild the working genome from scratch.
- **How it works:** The germline micronucleus contains scrambled gene segments; after conjugation the new somatic macronucleus is assembled by excising non-coding segments, reordering and inverting the remaining ones, and amplifying the products into "nanochromosomes", each typically carrying one gene. Small RNAs from the parental macronucleus guide which segments are kept and how they are ordered.
- **Numbers:** The macronuclear assembly covers about 16,000 complete nanochromosomes (one news account says about 15,600), total haploid genome about 50 Mb, nanochromosome lengths 469 bp to 66 kb (mean about 3.2 kb), encoding roughly 18,500 genes (Swart et al. 2013, *PLoS Biology*). The germline genome contains more than 3,500 scrambled genes and more than 800 predicted germline-limited genes, with segments for different somatic loci often interwoven (Chen et al. 2014, *Cell* 158:1187–1198). The often-quoted figure of "over 225,000 segments" appears in a news summary of the Cell paper rather than in the abstract, so treat it as unverified.
- **Energy and cost:** A staggering amount of regulated DNA surgery after every sexual cycle, plus the maintenance of two separate genomes with different architectures.
- **What triggers it:** Conjugation and the development of a new macronucleus.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** The reassembly is error-prone in principle and absolutely dependent on the small-RNA guidance from the parent, which makes the system a striking case of epigenetic inheritance. Why the germline is scrambled at all remains unexplained.
- **Confidence:** High for the architecture and the rearrangement process; medium for the 225,000-segment figure.
- **Sources:** https://db.cngb.org/data_resources/literature/25171416 ; https://doaj.org/article/2c997db6921e48499101458b87b25641

### 100. Lonely ecosystem powered by radioactivity
- **Category:** Anything else astonishing
- **Organism:** *Candidatus* Desulforudis audaxviator (bacterium)
- **What it does:** Nearly three kilometres underground, in water sealed in rock, researchers found an ecosystem made of essentially one organism. It lives with no light, no oxygen and no food from the surface, running on hydrogen split out of water by the radioactive decay of uranium and thorium in the surrounding rock.
- **How it works:** Sulfate reduction using hydrogen produced by radiolysis of water from the uranium and thorium decay chains; carbon is fixed from dissolved inorganic carbon, and nitrogen is fixed from the environment, so the organism needs no organic input at all. It can form endospores to protect its DNA.
- **Numbers:** Sampled at 2.8 km depth in the Mponeng gold mine, South Africa, from about 5,600 litres of fracture water. Over 99.9% of the recovered DNA came from this one organism, which has 2,157 protein-coding genes. Conditions: above 60 °C, pH about 9.3, no light or oxygen. Chivian et al. 2008, *Science* 322:275–278. Later studies reported it in West Siberia and in Death Valley, California.
- **Energy and cost:** The energy flux from radiolysis is tiny, so growth is presumably extremely slow; the organism carries a complete self-sufficient metabolism, which is a large genomic investment for such a small energy budget.
- **What triggers it:** Not applicable; this is its habitat.
- **Limits and weaknesses:** The "single-species ecosystem" label describes overwhelming dominance, not proven exclusivity, and the remainder of the DNA was attributed to mine and laboratory contamination — a judgement call. Doubling times in situ are not directly measured.
- **Confidence:** Medium-high. The genomic result is strong; "single-species ecosystem" is an interpretation, and no critique of it surfaced in the sources consulted.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desulforudis_audaxviator ; https://www.nature.com/news/2008/081009/full/news.2008.1160.html
