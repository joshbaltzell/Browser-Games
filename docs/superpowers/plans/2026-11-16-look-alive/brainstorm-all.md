# Look Alive: brainstorm, all ideas


## Lens 1

1. **The Mask Slider (Goal Lens)** (small, mvp). The dish HUD shows one big horizontal gauge titled COVER, with a green 'Passing' zone in the middle and red zones at both ends labelled 'Too Alien' and 'Too Perfect'. Your colony's marker sits on it live. The gauge replaces the idea of 'more is better' with a visibly two-sided target. *Why:* It makes the inverted win condition legible at a glance: the goal is a zone, not an extreme. *Risk:* Collapsing four channels into one gauge could hide which channel is the culprit, so it must link to the needles on hover.
2. **Win-Condition Postcard on Dish Entry** (small, mvp). Before each dish a single illustrated card shows a three-icon sentence: a small coloured dot inside a dashed band, a low suspicion needle, and a clock. Caption: 'Be 5 to 15% of the dish. Stay boring. Last 60 seconds.' Numbers are filled in per dish and the card slides to the corner as a permanent goal strip. *Why:* The goal is restated in the same visual language every dish, so no one has to remember it. *Risk:* Players skim intro cards, so it has to be tiny and persistent rather than a gate.
3. **Ghost Natives Tutorial (Be This)** (medium, mvp). In dish one your colony starts invisible and a translucent ghost population of natives plays a perfect run, tracing a faint curve on the graph. You raise one slider and your trace snaps toward the ghost curve. The line 'Match the ghost' is the only instruction. *Why:* Imitation is intuitive; players learn 'pass as native' by matching a visible target rather than reading about it. *Risk:* If the ghost curve is too easy to chase it teaches curve-matching rather than understanding channels.
4. **Lens Flip: Native View vs Your View** (medium, full). A hold-to-toggle key swaps the dish between 'Your Eyes' (you see your colony in friendly colour) and 'Immune Eyes' (a desaturated view where every cell is tinted by how far it deviates from native, with deviants glowing red). Hexagonal seams appear in immune view first. *Why:* It teaches the central idea, that suspicion is perception by someone else, using one button and no text. *Risk:* Needs a second shader pass and could push players to stay in one view and never learn the other.
5. **Autopsy as a Replay Pin** (medium, mvp). Instead of only text, the autopsy drops a red pin on the scrubbable timeline at the exact moment suspicion crossed a stage threshold, and auto-scrubs there. A caption reads 'Tick 212: waste pH left the native band. Natives noticed.' with the offending channel highlighted and a ghost of what a pass would look like. *Why:* Players see cause and effect in the replay itself rather than reading a verdict. *Risk:* If multiple causes overlap the single pin may blame the wrong one, so it must show the top two.
6. **One-Tap 'What If' Retry Chips** (medium, mvp). After an autopsy the screen offers two or three one-tap chips such as 'Waste -1 step' or 'Add 10% sloppiness'. Tapping applies the change and re-runs instantly, showing the before and after suspicion lines overlaid on the same graph. *Why:* It closes the loop from 'why did I fail' to 'what do I change' without making the player diagnose from scratch. *Risk:* Chips may become a crutch that solves puzzles for the player, so offer only one hint-level chip per failure.
7. **Verb Icons for Every Attribute** (medium, mvp). Each attribute and trade-off pair gets a pictographic icon and a verb label instead of a stat name: 'Multiply', 'Digest', 'Leak', 'Cling'. Sliding a pair shows a tiny looping microanimation of its effect on a sample cell. *Why:* Plain verbs and moving pictures explain what a stat does without a tooltip. *Risk:* Icons must stay distinct at small size, and microanimations add art and perf work per attribute.
8. **Sentence-Builder Rule Cards with Drag Chips** (medium, full). Reflex rule cards are built from three draggable word chips snapped into a sentence frame: 'When [food is low] then [go dormant]'. Chip colours match sensors (blue) and actions (orange), and the card reads aloud on hover in the handler's voice. *Why:* Programming looks like assembling a sentence, which removes the fear of scripting. *Risk:* Limited chip vocabulary can feel restrictive once players want compound conditions.
9. **The Handler's Margin Notes** (small, either). Every UI panel has an optional handwritten-style margin note from the handler explaining it wrongly but usefully, such as 'Suspicion: how much they dislike you. Be likeable.' A small toggle reveals the 'Corrected' note from a field-manual footnote with the accurate meaning. *Why:* Comedy carries the first explanation, and the footnote catches anyone the joke misled. *Risk:* Two notes per panel doubles the writing and the wrong advice may genuinely mislead players if the correction is hidden.
10. **Hexagon Tell as a Visible Meter** (small, mvp). The hex seams already shown on robot cells are tied to a clear rule: seam opacity equals the regularity channel. A small legend shows a smooth cell, a half-seamed cell, and a fully hex cell labelled 'Natural, Wobbly, Caught'. Tutorial dish one starts with seams obviously visible. *Why:* The robot tell becomes a readable instrument rather than decoration, and it teaches that 'too perfect' is the danger. *Risk:* If seams appear too early they spoil the organic look the art direction depends on.
11. **Dish Title as a Verb Phrase** (small, either). Each dish is named by its objective in plain action words, not a code: 'Stay Small, Eat Quietly', 'Don't Kill Anyone', 'Be Messy'. The title renders huge on the loading screen and the ladder map, with a small icon for each constraint it adds. *Why:* The dish name itself teaches the rule being practised and makes the ladder scannable. *Risk:* Short verb titles may spoil the puzzle if the dish is meant to be discovered.
12. **Planet Rule Banner with Flip Icon** (small, full). Each planet opens with a full-width banner showing the normal rule on the left and its inverted version on the right, connected by a flip arrow: 'Earth: noise is normal' to 'Here: silence is suspicious'. The inverted rule stays pinned as a small badge on every dish of that planet. *Why:* Players always know which assumption has been reversed, so planet changes feel like a rule, not a mystery. *Risk:* Pinned badge may be ignored and players keep applying the previous planet's habits.
13. **Normal-Band Breathing Hint** (small, mvp). On every channel needle the native normal range pulses softly like breathing and widens or narrows as the natives' own variance changes. A faint wobbling line shows that 'normal' itself moves. *Why:* It teaches that normal is a living range, which is the root of the regularity mechanic. *Risk:* Constant pulsing adds visual noise next to the already busy needles.
14. **Scrub-to-Cause Linked Highlighting** (large, full). While scrubbing the replay, hovering any suspicion spike lights the cells, rule cards and attributes responsible: rules flash gold, attribute sliders glow, and affected native cells outline in red. A thin line connects the spike to its source panel. *Why:* Cause and effect become spatial and visible, so no verbal explanation is needed. *Risk:* Tracking causal attribution through a field-level sim needs logged contributions, adding sim and memory cost.
15. **Cover Story Dossier** (medium, mvp). Each dish has a one-page dossier for your cover identity: a species card with a silhouette, a description of what natives do, and checkboxes the game ticks as you scan them, such as 'Eats slowly', 'Excretes mild acid', 'Fluctuates in size'. Your colony's own matching traits appear alongside as a comparison card. *Why:* It gives the player a plain-language checklist of what 'normal' is and what they match. *Risk:* Checklists could turn the puzzle into box-ticking if traits are too directly mapped to sliders.
16. **Failure Reframed as Cover Blown Screen** (medium, mvp). A failed run shows a stylised 'Cover Blown' dossier stamp with the dish's three stages as a comic strip of three panels: 'They got curious', 'They inspected', 'They responded', each annotated with the responsible channel. A single line below says 'Next time: be less X.' *Why:* Failure feels like a story beat with a clear takeaway, not a shrug. *Risk:* Comic panels require templated art that could feel repetitive after ten failures.
17. **Do-Nothing Gets Caught Tutorial Beat** (small, mvp). In dish two, a button labelled 'Just Sit There' is offered. Pressing it runs a short sim in which the colony dies out or goes conspicuously silent and the handler says 'Excellent. Nobody has ever noticed a dead colony.' The autopsy shows that doing nothing violates the population-share band. *Why:* It demonstrates that passivity fails by letting the player try it safely and learn why. *Risk:* If the button looks like a legit option players may use it as a cheat route in later dishes.
18. **Colour-Coded Cause Ribbon Under the Graph** (small, mvp). Beneath the population graph runs a thin ribbon that is coloured by whichever suspicion channel is currently the largest contributor, with a label every few seconds. A legend uses the same stain colours as the needles. *Why:* It answers 'what is hurting me right now' continuously during playback, so failure is never a surprise. *Risk:* Ribbon may flicker between channels when contributions are close, so it needs hysteresis.
19. **Teach-by-Contrast Twin Dishes** (large, full). Selected early dishes show two dishes side by side: the same build, one run on a planet with the normal rule and one with the inverted rule. Only one passes, and the screen highlights the one setting that differs. *Why:* A direct contrast teaches a planet rule faster than any description. *Risk:* Doubles sim cost per replay and needs careful dish authoring so the difference is clear.

## Lens 2

20. **Growth Curve Is the Tutorial** (small, mvp). The first dish shows a live graph of the natives alone: a lag phase, a steep log phase, then a flat plateau at carrying capacity. The player drags a single 'growth rate' slider and watches their own curve ghost over the native one. Plain labels sit on the three phases ('lag: cells getting ready', 'log: doubling', 'plateau: food is running out'). *Why:* Logistic growth is the engine of the whole sim, and seeing it as a shape teaches it faster than any definition. *Risk:* Players may read the plateau as 'the game stalls' and push growth to max, which is the exact overshoot we want them to learn from, so the failure has to be visible and cheap to retry.
21. **Overshoot and Crash Lesson Dish** (small, mvp). A scripted early dish where the obvious 'grow fast to hit the band' build overshoots carrying capacity, starves, and crashes. The replay highlights the nutrient field draining on the heat map, then the autopsy says 'you out-ate your dish'. The fix is a slower growth setting or a lower share target. *Why:* Teaches carrying capacity and boom-bust dynamics by letting the player fail in a safe, legible way. *Risk:* If the crash feels arbitrary rather than caused by the player's choice, it reads as a bug instead of a lesson.
22. **Niche Overlap Map** (medium, either). A scout-phase overlay that shows each food source on the dish as a coloured field and which natives eat which. The player's build has a 'diet' setting, and an overlap meter shows how much they compete with the dominant native. High overlap pushes the player out (competitive exclusion), zero overlap makes the colony look alien. *Why:* Makes niche theory and competitive exclusion a tangible dial: be similar enough to blend in, different enough not to get squeezed out. *Risk:* Two coupled effects (stealth versus survival) can feel like one confusing slider unless the UI separates 'blending' from 'competing'.
23. **Mimicry Is Imperfect Copying** (medium, full). Mimicry is a skill that copies the native's surface-marker colour pattern, shown as a ring of coloured dots on each cell. Copy fidelity costs resources, and low fidelity shows as a slightly wrong dot that an inspecting immune cell flags. The player decides which markers to copy, since copying all of them is too expensive. *Why:* Teaches molecular mimicry honestly: the immune system checks specific markers, and partial matches fail in specific ways. *Risk:* Marker puzzles can turn into a memory-matching minigame that overshadows the core loop.
24. **Self and Non-Self Tolerance Training** (medium, full). In the inspection stage, the immune cells are shown 'learning' from samples. If the colony shows up early and quietly, the cells learn it as normal and tolerance grows, shown as a rising 'tolerated' bar. Arriving late or in a burst means the immune system has already trained on pure native cells and you are foreign. *Why:* Teaches immune tolerance and why early, gradual colonisation behaves differently from a late invasion. *Risk:* Tolerance as a timer can make the opening seconds feel decisive and pressure the player to restart constantly.
25. **Quorum Sensing Threshold Tuner** (medium, full). The quorum signal skill shows signal molecules as a faint fog on the grid. Below a density threshold the colony stays silent, above it a coordinated behaviour switches on, such as a biofilm or a toxin release, shown as a sudden glow across the colony. The player sets the threshold on a slider and sees where the switch would fire on the graph. *Why:* Quorum sensing becomes a decision about when to act together, and the sudden switch-on shows why density matters. *Risk:* A sudden global switch can cause an obvious spike in the regularity suspicion channel, so players may think quorum is a trap and avoid it.
26. **Biofilm as Armour with a Waste Bill** (small, full). Biofilm is drawn as a translucent matrix that thickens around the colony and shields it from the immune stage 3 response. The cost is shown as a slowed growth rate and a lingering waste-chemistry signature that natives do not produce. The player sees both the shield value and the bill before committing. *Why:* Biofilms really do protect and really do cost; making the trade-off explicit teaches both halves. *Risk:* If biofilm is clearly the best option in every dish, players learn 'always biofilm' instead of the trade-off.
27. **Chemostat Dish Type** (medium, full). A special dish where nutrients flow in and waste and cells flow out at a constant dilution rate, drawn as a drip at the top and drain at the bottom. The natives sit at a steady state, and the player must hold their share steady while the flow rate changes mid-run. *Why:* Introduces the chemostat, the real lab tool for holding populations steady, and turns it into a different feel from batch dishes. *Risk:* A steady-state dish can feel static and slow if the flow changes are not dramatic enough.
28. **Horizontal Gene Transfer Swap Event** (large, full). A mid-dish event where a native cell swaps a trait, shown as a glowing thread between two cells. The player can accept the swap and gain a native trait (such as the native waste profile) at the cost of the colony picking up a native weakness too. The trait appears as a new card in the build panel. *Why:* Shows how real bacteria share genes sideways, and gives mimicry a second route beyond building it. *Risk:* Needs a trait system that can be added and removed mid-run, which complicates the deterministic sim and the build code format.
29. **Antibiotic Pressure Dish** (medium, full). A hostile planet dish where a drug field drifts across the grid in waves. The colony can survive with a resistance attribute, but resistance costs growth, and the dose history in the graph shows the classic pattern: most die, a few survive, those few regrow resistant. The replay colours resistant cells a different stain. *Why:* Teaches selection and resistance as population change, not individual adaptation, which is the most commonly misunderstood idea. *Risk:* Drug-resistance framing may read as a real-world medical message, so the copy must stay clearly in the alien setting.
30. **Stain Lab Mini-Scenes** (small, either). Before each planet, a short interactive Gram-stain-style scene: the player applies crystal violet, then iodine, then a decolouriser, then safranin, and watches cells turn purple or pink depending on their wall type. The colour legend in the main game then references what each stain showed. *Why:* Makes the stain colours that decorate the whole game mean something real and gives the legend a reason to exist. *Risk:* A real staining procedure is procedural and could feel like a chore if repeated every planet.
31. **Scale Bar and Doubling Clock** (small, mvp). A permanent scale bar and a small clock showing 'one doubling = N minutes' on the dish, with labels that change as the player zooms. Zooming from the whole dish to a single cell shows the size ladder: cell, bacterium, virus, with the robot cells sized honestly beside them. *Why:* Builds a gut sense of microscopic scale and time, which is the foundation of everything else. *Risk:* Honest scale makes cells tiny and hard to see, which fights the glowing readable look.
32. **Where the Game Lies** (small, either). A 'Lab Notes' codex entry for each mechanic with two lines: what the science says, and what the game changed (for example 'real quorum signals take hours; here, seconds'). The entry unlocks the first time the player uses the mechanic, and the handler adds a deadpan comment about the simplification. *Why:* Being upfront about simplifications builds trust and keeps the game educational rather than misleading. *Risk:* Players skip text, so the notes need to be short and delivered at the moment of use, not in a menu.
33. **Prediction Before Replay** (medium, full). Before the simulation resolves, the player drags a rough sketch of the curve they expect onto the graph, then the real run plays over it. A small 'prediction score' appears afterwards, and a big gap triggers a plain explanation of the biggest surprise. *Why:* Prediction before outcome is the strongest proven way to make people notice their own misconceptions. *Risk:* It adds a step before every retry, which could slow the fast tweak-and-retry loop that makes the game fun.
34. **Counterfactual Ghost Run** (medium, full). After the autopsy, one button replays the same dish with the single cause removed, such as 'what if you had skipped the waste spike?', drawn as a ghost trace over the original. The player sees directly how much that one factor mattered. *Why:* A controlled single-variable comparison is how science works, and it makes the autopsy a lesson in cause and effect. *Risk:* The ghost run may be misleading if factors interact, so it must say it removed only one thing and others stayed fixed.
35. **Native Field Journal** (medium, either). Each native species gets a journal page that fills in as the player scouts it: growth rate, preferred food, waste signature, regularity range, and a hand-drawn-looking procedural sketch. The page only lists what has been measured, with blank rows for what has not. *Why:* Turns scouting into collecting real ecological facts and gives players a reference they can check when a build fails. *Risk:* If the journal holds all the answers, players may skip scouting and just read it, which removes the discovery.
36. **Variance Is Biology** (small, mvp). A short scouting prompt shows two native colonies with identical averages but different spread, and the player sees that every real population has noise, shown as a cloud around the mean on every graph. The regularity channel is introduced here as 'real life is messy', with the player adding noise on purpose. *Why:* Teaches that biological populations vary, and why a perfect trace is the giveaway, using statistics the player can see. *Risk:* Noise sliders invite the degenerate 'max randomness' strategy, so too much noise must also read as suspicious.
37. **Succession Chapter** (large, full). A late-game chapter where the dish is seeded with pioneer species, then changes over several stages as the first arrivals alter the environment (pH, oxygen) and make room for later species. The player must arrive at the right stage and fit the species of that stage. *Why:* Teaches ecological succession and that timing of arrival matters as much as traits. *Risk:* Multi-stage dishes need a lot of hand-tuned content and can blow the scope.

## Lens 3

38. **The Seam Reveal** (medium, mvp). When suspicion crosses a threshold, a slow radial wipe sweeps across the dish and the organic blobs briefly resolve into crisp hexagonal tiles, then relax back. In the replay it plays at the exact moment of exposure, held for half a second with the audio dropping out. *Why:* It is the one-image summary of the whole premise (a robot caught pretending), and it is instantly legible in a screenshot or GIF. *Risk:* If the seams look like a generic honeycomb filter instead of a reveal, the signature moment reads as a cheap effect.
39. **Two-Frame Thumbnail Rule** (small, either). Design every dish so the capsule image and key art are a split lens: left half shows glowing round natives, right half shows the same cells with hex seams, with a single violet-pink cell sitting in the middle. Use the same split composition on the title screen, store capsule, and social card. *Why:* A stranger scrolling past understands 'one of these is fake' in under a second, which sells the inverted goal without text. *Risk:* Spoils the hex-seam reveal if it becomes the only image shown everywhere, leaving nothing fresh in the game itself.
40. **Specimen Label Title Screen** (small, mvp). The title screen is a glass slide on a dark stage: a handwritten-style museum label reads 'Bacillus Normalis (n=4,000,000) - Not a robot', with the logo letters made of rod-shaped cells that drift and occasionally flicker a hex seam. Pressing start slides the slide under the lens and focus-pulls into the first dish. *Why:* The label joke states the pitch and the comedy tone before any tutorial, and the focus-pull gives a satisfying, clippable start. *Risk:* Procedurally drawn rod-cell lettering may be hard to keep readable at small sizes.
41. **Lens Focus-Pull Transitions** (small, mvp). Every scene change (dish select, replay start, autopsy) is a microscope focus pull: the image goes blurry with big bokeh blobs and snaps sharp, with a slight chromatic fringe and a soft synth rack-focus sound. The replay begins zoomed out and dives in. *Why:* Gives a consistent motion language that feels like instrument use rather than menu UI, and looks great in trailers. *Risk:* Repeated transitions on every retry become annoying if they cannot be skipped or shortened.
42. **Stain Palette as Brand and Rule** (small, mvp). Lock the four stain colours to meanings used everywhere: violet is natives, pink is you, amber is waste, green is immune activity. Logo, UI, store page and social cards use only these on black, so any screenshot is recognisable as the game by colour alone. *Why:* A tight palette makes the game identifiable in a thumbnail and doubles as a learnable key. *Risk:* Colour-blind players may struggle with violet versus pink, so shape or pattern backups are required.
43. **Photo Mode: Take a Micrograph** (medium, full). A camera button in the replay freezes the frame, lets the player drag a focus point and zoom, and exports a PNG styled as a journal micrograph with scale bar, stain label, magnification, and a deadpan caption generated from the run (for example 'Fig. 3: Subject attempting to look casual'). *Why:* Turns the prettiest moments into shareable images the players make themselves, which is free marketing. *Risk:* Canvas export from a WebGL view on file:// can hit tainted-canvas or preserveDrawingBuffer problems that need a spike.
44. **Autopsy as Annotated Slide** (medium, mvp). The failure autopsy is drawn onto a still of the dish like a lab slide: arrow lines with handwritten notes point at the exact cells and moment that gave you away, with a red circle around the tell and a one-line deadpan verdict. *Why:* It is more screenshot-worthy than a text panel, and the shareable image explains the failure to someone who has never played. *Risk:* Placing annotations without overlapping cells or running off screen needs careful layout logic.
45. **Cover Name Stamp** (small, mvp). Each dish ends with a passport-style stamp slammed down on the specimen card: 'ACCEPTED AS Bacillus Normalis' in stain ink, with a satisfying thud and slight screen shake. Failed dishes get a different stamp, 'DETECTED', slightly crooked. *Why:* A strong, repeatable victory beat that is quick to clip and builds a collection feel. *Risk:* Stamp overuse could make wins feel like paperwork rather than a payoff.
46. **Passing Score as a Number You Brag About** (small, mvp). Replace the generic score with a single big 'Native %' that reads like a purity badge, shown huge on the result card and in share images, for example 'PASSED AS NATIVE: 94%'. *Why:* A single bold number is the easiest thing to post and compare, and it explains the goal in three words. *Risk:* Players may chase the number rather than learning the systems, encouraging slider-sweeping.
47. **Rack Focus Trailer Sequence** (medium, full). Design a 30-second trailer built from in-game footage: a calm dish of natives, the camera pushes in on one cell, a handler voice-line cheerfully says 'Just act natural', hex seams flicker, immune cells swarm, hard cut to black, title card. Build a trailer-recording mode that hides the UI and auto-plays a scripted replay. *Why:* Gives a clear, funny hook shaped for the store page and social video, using only existing rendering. *Risk:* A scripted showcase needs a polished dish and tuned replay timing, which may not exist until late.
48. **Colony Needle Logo** (small, mvp). The logo wordmark 'LOOK ALIVE' is set in a round humanist type with each letter filled with swirling cells, and the 'I' is a single rod bacterium with a tiny blinking light. In-game, the logo's glow pulses in time with the suspicion drone. *Why:* A simple, ownable mark that works as a favicon and avatar, and quietly signals the robot theme. *Risk:* The blinking-light detail can look like a tech brand logo rather than a living thing if overdone.
49. **Tell Cells Glow When You Look Away** (small, mvp). Idle on the title screen or pause menu and a few cells in the background slowly develop hex seams, then hide them again when the mouse moves. A tiny caption beneath says 'They only notice when you stop moving.' *Why:* Creates an unexpectedly eerie and charming first impression, and gives idle viewers a hook. *Risk:* Subtle effects can be missed entirely on a first visit.
50. **Handler Letterhead Cards** (small, mvp). Handler messages arrive as sheets on cream letterhead ('Department of Plausible Deniability') dropped on the dark dish, with coffee rings, a stamped 'URGENT', and a bad hand-drawn diagram of a cell. The contrast between warm paper and cold microscope makes each message a distinct screenshot. *Why:* Warm paper against black is a strong visual contrast and builds the comedy world with almost no art. *Risk:* Paper texture drawn in code can look flat unless carefully done with noise.
51. **Dish Collection Wall** (medium, full). A home screen showing every cleared dish as a small glowing circle on a dark wall of Petri dishes, each tinted with the stain palette and labelled with its cover name. Cleared dishes pulse softly and unplayed ones are dim. *Why:* Reads as a single beautiful image of progress, which works as a screenshot and a reason to return. *Risk:* Many small live dishes can hurt performance if each runs its own render.
52. **Scale Bar Zoom Reveal** (large, full). The replay camera can dive from the whole dish to a single cell in one continuous zoom with a live scale bar updating from millimetres to micrometres. At the deepest zoom, you see one cell with a faint hex seam, inside which is a tiny readable line of text such as a serial number. *Why:* A wow moment that shows off the field-to-individual simulation and gives a perfect trailer shot. *Risk:* Seamless zoom across representation levels is technically hard and can reveal where individuals are faked.
53. **Planet Postcard Per Chapter** (medium, full). Each planet has a one-colour tinted postcard backdrop with a stamp and a wonky greeting ('Wish you were not detected'), used as the chapter select screen and store art. The planet's inverted rule is printed on the stamp as a tagline. *Why:* Gives each chapter an instantly distinct identity in screenshots and a funny way to introduce the rule inversion. *Risk:* Needs distinct visuals per planet, which pushes against the mostly-procedural art constraint.
54. **Native Pride Gallery of Fake Faces** (medium, full). A silly collectibles screen: every cover name you pass as unlocks a 'Native of the Month' card with a procedurally generated cell portrait (googly spots, flagella, a hat made from a shape) and a deadpan bio. Cards can be exported as images. *Why:* Character and humour from pure procedural generation, and a very shareable unit. *Risk:* Cute portraits may drift the tone away from the eerie dark-field look.
55. **Bloom and Vignette Mood Meter** (small, mvp). Tie the lens itself to suspicion: at low suspicion the vignette is soft and the bloom warm; as suspicion rises the vignette tightens, the picture desaturates slightly and a faint scanline grid appears, until the final stage turns the image cold and hard-edged. *Why:* The screen itself tells the story at a glance, which makes any screenshot readable as 'this player is in trouble'. *Risk:* Heavy mood changes can reduce legibility exactly when players need to read the dish.
56. **Replay Clip Button with Looping GIF Output** (medium, full). A one-click 'Clip it' button records the last five seconds of the replay canvas (the exposure moment or the pass stamp) and offers a looping WebM or frames-as-GIF download with the share caption baked in. *Why:* Removes friction from posting the five-second clip, which is the most likely marketing channel. *Risk:* MediaRecorder on canvas from a local file may behave inconsistently across browsers.

## Lens 4

57. **One-tap Retry With Last Build** (small, mvp). After the replay ends (or at any time during it), a big Retry button re-runs the dish with the previous build still loaded and the single most-blamed setting highlighted with a pulsing ring. Retry takes one tap and the sim resolves instantly, so the next replay starts within a second. *Why:* Makes the tweak-and-retry loop feel like a flick, not a menu trip. *Risk:* Players may mash retry without reading the autopsy, so the highlight must be tied to the autopsy's named cause or it teaches nothing.
58. **Ghost Run Overlay** (small, mvp). The previous attempt's population curve and per-channel suspicion lines stay on the graph as faint dashed ghosts while the new run plays. The player sees at a glance whether the tweak moved the needle, and where the two runs diverge. *Why:* Gives instant feedback on whether a change helped, with no memory or note-taking. *Risk:* Clutters the graph on small screens, so it needs a toggle and only one ghost, not a history.
59. **Slider Preview Needles** (medium, full). While dragging a build slider, the suspicion needles show a translucent predicted position computed from a cheap one-pass estimate of the sim (not the full run). Release the slider and the needle ghost snaps to the committed value. The preview is labelled 'rough guess' in the handler's voice. *Why:* Turns sliders from blind guesses into directly steerable controls, shortening the loop before the first run. *Risk:* If the estimate disagrees with the real run, players distrust it, so it must be conservative and visibly approximate.
60. **Tap-to-Fill Rule Sentences** (medium, mvp). A rule card is a sentence with underlined blanks: 'WHEN [sensor] is [higher/lower than] [value] THEN [action]'. Tapping a blank opens a small radial picker with 4 to 6 icon-and-word choices, large enough for a thumb. No typing and no dragging of logic blocks. *Why:* Makes programming feel like filling in a Mad Lib, so scripting does not scare players off. *Risk:* Limited choices per blank may feel restrictive for veterans, so unlocks must widen the option sets over time.
61. **Rule Card Dry Fire** (medium, full). Each rule card has a small test button that fast-forwards the sim to the first moment the card would fire and shows a one-line result: 'Fired at 0:14, released 30% biofilm'. If it never fires, the card says so and shows the sensor's actual range during that run. *Why:* Answers the most common scripting question, 'did my rule even do anything?', without a full replay. *Risk:* Needs the sim to log per-rule trigger events, which constrains how the engine is structured early.
62. **Rule Starter Presets** (small, mvp). Empty rule slots offer three pre-written suggested cards drawn from the handler ('Back off when the natives get crowded'). One tap drops the card in, and its blanks stay editable. Suggestions are chosen from the current dish's known problem, not a fixed list. *Why:* Removes the blank-page problem for the first scripts and teaches rule shape by example. *Risk:* Players may only ever use presets, so presets should be deliberately slightly wrong for some dishes, in keeping with the handler's bad advice.
63. **Autopsy Jump-To-Moment** (small, mvp). Every line in the failure autopsy is a link. Tapping 'Waste spiked at 0:42' scrubs the replay to that moment, zooms the view onto the offending region, and pauses with the relevant channel highlighted. *Why:* Connects the written cause to the visible evidence in one tap, so players understand failures without hunting through the timeline. *Risk:* If the scrub target is slightly off, the evidence looks wrong and the autopsy loses credibility.
64. **Pattern-Coded Channels** (small, mvp). Every suspicion channel and stain colour also carries a distinct pattern or glyph: growth is stripes, waste is dots, casualties is crosshatch, regularity is a hex outline. The same pattern appears on needles, timeline bands, legend, and autopsy text. A colour-blind mode swaps the stain palette for a safe set but keeps the glyphs. *Why:* Makes the game readable for colour-blind players without losing the stain-colour aesthetic. *Risk:* Patterns on glowing translucent cells could fight the dark-field look, so they are limited to UI, not the cells.
65. **Calm Lens Mode** (small, mvp). A single accessibility toggle that stops screen shake, bloom pulsing, flicker, and vignette breathing, replaces fast cell wiggle with slow drift, and turns the audio drone's tremolo into a steady tone. State changes are shown with shape and text instead of flashes. Rule-fire flashes become a static outline. *Why:* Respects reduced-motion and photosensitive players with one switch, rather than burying options in menus. *Risk:* If every effect is hard-coded, retrofitting is painful, so effects must route through one intensity variable from day one.
66. **Lens Size and Contrast Dial** (medium, full). A small dial on the HUD scales all UI text and needle size from 100% to 200%, and a second raises the contrast of cell outlines against the black field. Layouts reflow so nothing overlaps at the largest size. *Why:* Supports low-vision players and phone users, since a microscope aesthetic tends toward tiny glowing text. *Risk:* Large text breaks hand-tuned layouts, so a responsive layout system is required from the start.
67. **Thumb Zone Layout** (medium, full). On phones, the build and rule screens are arranged for one-handed play: the sliders and cards sit in the lower half, the dish view in the upper half, and the Run button floats at the bottom right. The replay scrubber is a wide bar along the bottom edge with a big handle. *Why:* Makes the game playable on a train, with one hand, without precision taps. *Risk:* Designing two layouts doubles the QA, so one flexible layout that rotates its panels is safer than two.
68. **Pinch-Scan Lens** (medium, mvp). Scouting is done by a circular lens the player drags over the dish. Pinch (or scroll wheel) widens or narrows it. Inside the lens, natives show their labelled readouts (growth rate, waste, spacing) as small text tags. Outside, the dish is plain. No separate scan mode or menu is needed. *Why:* Makes scouting direct and physical, and cuts a menu step out of learning what normal is. *Risk:* Players may not discover the pinch, so the default size must be useful and the gesture hinted once.
69. **Normal Band Pin** (small, mvp). The player can pin any observed native readout (for example 'native growth: 3 to 5 per minute') to a strip at the top of the build screen. Pinned bands sit next to the sliders they affect, so the target range is visible while adjusting. *Why:* Saves the player from memorising numbers or flipping between scouting and build screens. *Risk:* Pinning too many bands makes a cluttered strip, so cap it at three or four slots.
70. **Undo Stack And Build Snapshots** (small, mvp). Every slider move and card placement goes on an undo stack (Z or a two-finger tap). A row of three snapshot slots lets the player save the current build and swap back instantly, shown as small thumbnails of each build's result graph. *Why:* Lets players experiment boldly, since mistakes cost one tap instead of a rebuild. *Risk:* Snapshot slots invite hoarding builds instead of reasoning, but three slots are a small enough cap.
71. **Auto-Sloppy Dial** (small, mvp). Instead of hand-tuning each timing, the regularity channel gets one 'Messiness' slider with a live noise preview (a small wavy line that shows how irregular the colony's timing will be). The autopsy for regularity failures points at this dial by name. Sensible default is a little above zero. *Why:* Turns the hardest concept, deliberate sloppiness, into a single legible control rather than several hidden parameters. *Risk:* One dial makes the regularity solution too easy, so it should cost something, such as slower growth.
72. **Keyboard And Gamepad Pass** (medium, full). Every control is reachable by tab or arrow keys with a visible focus ring styled as a lens reticle. Number keys pick rule blanks, space runs, R retries, brackets scrub the replay one frame at a time, and an on-screen key hint strip can be toggled. Gamepad d-pad maps to the same actions. *Why:* Gives fast players a very short retry loop and supports players who cannot use a pointer. *Risk:* Focus order bugs are easy to introduce, so it needs a short manual test pass on every new screen.
73. **Spoken Field Report Captions** (small, full). All handler lines and field reports are shown as captions and can optionally be read out loud by the browser's built-in speech synthesis, with a speed control. Caption text is large, high contrast, and can be pinned open instead of fading. *Why:* Supports low-vision and dyslexic players without needing any recorded voice work. *Risk:* Built-in voices vary by device and can sound grating, so it must be off by default and clearly optional.
74. **Sound-Of-Suspicion Visual Twin** (small, mvp). Every audio cue, such as the suspicion drone's pitch, immune stage chimes, and rule-fire clicks, has a visual twin: a thin pulsing ring around the dish edge and a captioned icon. A mute toggle leaves the game fully playable and equally informative. *Why:* Makes the game work on mute (common on phones and at work) and for deaf or hard-of-hearing players. *Risk:* Doubling every cue risks visual noise, so the ring should be thin and share one channel.
75. **Fast-Forward Replay With Skip To Trouble** (small, mvp). The replay scrubber has speed buttons (1x, 4x, 16x) and a 'Skip to trouble' button that jumps to the first moment any suspicion channel crossed a warning threshold, or to the end if none did. Auto-play defaults to 4x on retries after the first. *Why:* Cuts the 'watching, not playing' time on repeat attempts while keeping the first viewing leisurely. *Risk:* Players who always skip may miss the comedy and the slow reveal, so the first run of each dish plays at normal speed.

## Lens 5

76. **Sloppiness dial with a live jitter budget** (medium, full). A single 'Noise' meter sits next to the regularity needle. During the replay the player can see a thin ribbon of their colony's timing trace and the natives' trace overlaid; the player spends a limited Sloppiness budget (e.g. 3 charges per dish) by dragging a brush across the timeline to inject deliberate variance into specific windows (a late division, a missed pulse). The sim re-resolves instantly. *Why:* Turns the regularity channel into a tactile, expressive verb and makes 'be deliberately bad at it' a funny, learnable skill rather than a slider. *Risk:* Players may brush noise everywhere until regularity reads flat, so the budget must be tight and noise must cost growth efficiency.
77. **Rehearsal Ghost Runs** (small, mvp). Every attempt is saved as a faint ghost line on the population graph and suspicion timeline. The player sees their last three attempts overlaid and can click any ghost point to see which setting differed. A 'Best Cover' ghost persists per dish. *Why:* Makes iteration feel like mastery with visible progress, and speeds learning of cause and effect between tweaks. *Risk:* Graph clutter on small screens if more than three ghosts are shown.
78. **Live Intervention Pings** (large, full). While the replay plays (not before), the player has two or three one-shot 'field pings' per dish: Hush (suppress waste for 5 seconds), Scatter (disperse colony to lower local density), Play Dead (freeze growth briefly). They click during a suspicion spike and the sim re-resolves from that tick onward, with the replay forking visibly. *Why:* Gives the player something to do while the simulation runs and creates tension and release moments, fixing the 'watching, not playing' risk. *Risk:* Breaks the pure 'program it and watch' fantasy and complicates determinism and build codes unless pings are recorded as part of the build.
79. **Native Specimen Hunt** (medium, either). During scouting, a magnifier cursor lets the player tag individual natives doing something odd (a native that divides early, one that dies at random, a long-lived outlier). Each tagged oddity adds a 'permitted weirdness' entry to the normal band, widening a channel's tolerance slightly and giving a cover excuse. *Why:* Rewards curiosity and close observation, makes scouting an active game instead of a read-only panel, and gives experts legitimate edge-case exploits. *Risk:* If the oddities are hard to spot at the dish's zoom level the mechanic frustrates; needs obvious glow cues after the first dish.
80. **Inspector Probe Minigame** (medium, full). When the immune response reaches the inspection stage, a short interactive beat plays: a probe sweeps across the dish and the player gets a 5 second window to tap a few of their own cells to 'blend them in' behind a native cluster. Success lowers the inspection spike; failure shows the hex seams peeking out. *Why:* Creates the single most tense, readable moment in the game and a release when you scrape through. *Risk:* Twitch input may feel out of tone for a thoughtful puzzle game; keep it generous and skippable with an assist setting.
81. **Constraint Mutators (Dish Modifiers)** (small, full). Each cleared dish unlocks an optional modifier card the player can slap on a replay for a higher rank: 'No Mimicry', 'Rule Slots Locked at 1', 'Casualties Must Be Zero', 'Budget Minus 2', 'Blind Scouting (no scan)'. Completed modifiers stamp the dish card with a pip. *Why:* Gives replayability and expert depth without writing new dishes, and punishes degenerate strategies by removing the crutch each one relies on. *Risk:* Some modifier combos may be unwinnable, so each needs a quick solver or sim check before shipping.
82. **Par-Style Elegance Score** (small, either). Each dish shows a par for 'moves used': total attribute points shifted, rule cards placed, skills bought. Beating par earns a lab-coat badge; the score line reads 'Solved with 2 rule cards and a regretful amount of biofilm'. *Why:* Rewards minimal, clever solutions, discourages slider sweeping, and gives experts a clear target. *Risk:* Par may push players toward brittle solutions that pass only by a hair.
83. **Anti-Flatline Rule: The Dead Giveaway** (small, mvp). If a colony's population or suspicion graph is too flat or too smooth (low variance across the whole run), a new suspicion channel called Regularity spikes and the autopsy shows a stamp 'Statistically Impossible Consistency'. Natives' graphs in each dish carry authored wobble that the player must roughly match in amplitude. *Why:* Directly defeats the sweep-sliders-until-flat degenerate strategy and the do-nothing strategy, and makes the funniest failure message in the game. *Risk:* If the wobble match is opaque, it feels like an arbitrary rule; the normal-band overlay must show the wobble envelope.
84. **Do-Nothing Is a Fingerprint** (small, mvp). A colony with default settings and no rules gets flagged by a 'Factory Defaults' tell: early dishes include a native that checks for the exact stock-build signature, so doing nothing yields a specific named exposure ('Out-of-box specimen detected'). *Why:* Closes the do-nothing exploit and turns it into a joke the first time players try it. *Risk:* Needs the tutorial dish to exempt this, or new players feel punished for not knowing yet.
85. **Dish Wagers** (medium, full). Before pressing Run, the player can optionally wager a lab-grant token on a stated prediction: 'I expect peak suspicion under 40%' or 'I will finish with 8 to 10% share'. A correct call doubles the tokens; a wrong one costs some. Tokens buy cosmetic stain palettes and handler outfits. *Why:* Forces the player to form a mental model before the replay, adds anticipation, and rewards understanding rather than luck. *Risk:* Players may lowball predictions to farm tokens; use a tiered payout based on prediction tightness.
86. **Surprise Native Behaviours** (medium, either). Every dish has one authored 'event' that shows in the replay: a bacteriophage burst, a sudden nutrient crash, a native species mass-sporulating, a pH dip. The player is told only that 'something happens around tick 60', and their build must be robust to it. A cheap forecast icon can be bought by scouting. *Why:* Adds surprise and variety of challenge, and teaches robustness over tuning to one trajectory. *Risk:* Events that feel unfair or unforeseeable cause rage quits; scouting must always be able to reveal them.
87. **Rule Card Mutations** (medium, full). Each time a rule card fires in a replay, it gets a small wear mark. After enough use, the player can 'evolve' a card by choosing between two tweaks: a looser threshold, or an added cooldown. Evolved cards show a different border and a one-line footnote joke. *Why:* Gives rule cards progression and personality, makes scarce slots feel precious, and adds mid-game depth. *Risk:* More card variants add UI and balance work and can overwhelm players already wary of scripting.
88. **Scripted Rival Colony** (large, full). From the middle chapters, a second foreign robot colony shares the dish (a rival infiltrator from another agency) and its behaviour is visibly sloppy. If the immune system catches the rival, suspicion spills over onto you unless you distance your signature from it. *Why:* Adds a new type of challenge where the player manages guilt by association, and gives comedic material about incompetent colleagues. *Risk:* Needs an extra deterministic agent type and careful balance so it is never just noise.
89. **Reverse Dish: Be the Native** (large, full). An occasional puzzle flips the goal: the player controls a native strain and must get the robot colony to look suspicious to the immune system by nudging the dish (a short 'expose the spy' chapter). Same engine, inverted win condition. *Why:* Breaks up the loop with a surprising role reversal and deepens the player's understanding of suspicion channels. *Risk:* Needs its own onboarding and might confuse players who just learned the main goal.
90. **Cause-and-Effect Scrub Hotspots** (medium, mvp). While scrubbing the replay, the timeline auto-marks 'moments of interest' (first spike, near miss, recovery) with small flags. Clicking a flag pauses and highlights the cells and rule that caused it, and offers a 'try changing this' button that jumps straight to the relevant control. *Why:* Makes the replay interactive and teaches by showing causes, closing the loop from watching to tweaking. *Risk:* Bad flag detection could mislead players; use only the top 3 deterministic events.
91. **Cover Story Collection** (small, full). Each dish solved with a distinct approach records a 'cover story' in a field notebook: mimic, hide-in-biofilm, outbreed-then-retreat. The notebook has set bingo-style goals ('solve one dish using only waste chemistry'). Completing a row unlocks a cosmetic or a harder variant. *Why:* Encourages trying varied strategies and gives a light long-term goal that expert players can chase. *Risk:* Detecting which approach was used needs reliable heuristics or the labels feel wrong.
92. **Mission Debrief Replay Cut** (medium, full). After a win, the game auto-assembles a 10 second highlight clip from the replay (closest call, rule firing, final band hold) with a handler voiceover line and a stamped 'PASSED' frame. The player can loop it, and it is rendered live in the page, no recording needed. *Why:* Delivers a satisfying release after tension and creates a natural moment to share, without any server. *Risk:* Choosing a good clip automatically is fiddly; a fixed template with three scripted camera moves is safer.
93. **Sabotage Dishes (Fix a Failing Build)** (small, either). A recurring puzzle type hands the player a pre-built colony that is already failing and a single autopsy line. The player gets only two edits to rescue it. No blank-slate building, just diagnosis under constraint. *Why:* Adds variety of challenge, teaches reading the autopsy, and is a quick, low-commitment palette cleanser between big dishes. *Risk:* If the diagnosis is too obvious it feels trivial; seed with two plausible red herrings.

## Lens 6

94. **The Handler's Wrong Advice Ledger** (small, mvp). Before each dish the handler sends a briefing with one confident tip that is subtly wrong, such as 'Bacteria prefer it when you glow brighter than them.' After the autopsy, a ledger line reports whether you followed it: 'Advice followed: yes. Result: exposed. Handler comment: noted.' Some tips are secretly right, so the player must scout to judge. *Why:* Turns the handler from flavour text into a running gag that also teaches players to verify with scouting instead of trusting instructions. *Risk:* If the wrong advice is truly misleading it feels like a trick, so each tip must be provably checkable from the scout data.
95. **Handler Morale Meter** (small, either). The handler's messages degrade or brighten based on your record: after three clean passes he takes credit ('Our strategy is working'), after failures he reassigns blame ('Per my earlier note, you deviated'). A tiny portrait is a typed-out ASCII face that changes expression. *Why:* Gives the story a reactive voice and a reason to feel something about repeated retries, all from a text table keyed to run history. *Risk:* Branching lines multiply writing work, so keep it to about five mood tiers.
96. **Cover Name Generator With Consequences** (small, mvp). Each build gets an auto-generated Latin cover name from its attributes: a motile, low-waste colony becomes 'Bacillus Unremarkabilis', a sloppy one 'Coccus Vagabundus'. The name appears on the dish label, the field report and the autopsy, and a failed run is logged as 'Bacillus Unremarkabilis (deceased, publicly)'. *Why:* Makes every build feel like a character with zero art cost, and gives players something to screenshot and share. *Risk:* Needs a large enough word list to avoid obvious repeats by dish ten.
97. **Autopsy Written as a Coroner's Memo** (small, mvp). The failure autopsy opens with a deadpan one-line verdict in bureaucratic voice that names the channel: 'Cause of exposure: your colony divided at exactly 40.00-minute intervals. Natives are not punctual.' Underneath sit the real numbers and the timeline marker where it went wrong. *Why:* Players cannot tell why they failed; a funny, specific sentence is both the clearest explanation and the most memorable one. *Risk:* Jokes must never replace the actual diagnosis, so the plain cause line has to stay visible next to the joke.
98. **Regularity Is Funny: The Metronome Tell** (medium, mvp). When the regularity channel spikes, a faint synth metronome tick fades in under the drone, and the player sees every robot cell pulse in visible unison like a marching band while natives drift. A caption reads 'Colony observed to be in step.' Adding sloppiness visibly turns the march into a shamble. *Why:* Makes the hardest-to-grasp channel instantly legible as a joke you see and hear, and makes adding variance feel like a punchline. *Risk:* Unison pulsing must be done in the shader on aggregate phase, or it will cost performance at instanced scale.
99. **The Sloppiness Dial Named 'Vibes'** (small, mvp). The deliberate-variance control is labelled 'Authentic Sloppiness' with a dial that has deadpan tick labels from 'Spreadsheet' through 'Teenager' to 'Chaos Gremlin'. Turning it up makes the preview wobble, and the field report praises you: 'Colony now indistinguishable from mess.' *Why:* Reframes the awkward robot fix of being intentionally imperfect as the game's central joke, and makes the mechanic memorable and teachable. *Risk:* Cute labels can hide real numeric meaning, so the actual variance value must stay shown beside the joke label.
100. **Rule Cards Read in Handler Dialect** (small, either). Reflex cards show the plain sentence, plus a toggled-on 'Original Firmware' line beneath in robotic bureaucratese: 'IF neighbours > 40% THEN divide' becomes 'UPON DETECTION OF EXCESS NEIGHBOURS, INITIATE UNREQUESTED MITOSIS.' When a card fires, a tiny voice-box blip like a dial-up modem plays. *Why:* Plays on the premise that the rules are literally the robot's programming and softens scripting anxiety by making cards feel characterful, not technical. *Risk:* Second line adds visual clutter on small cards, so it should be optional and off by default.
101. **Native Wildlife Documentary Narrator** (medium, mvp). During the scout phase, a calm nature-documentary voice appears as text captions over the dark field: 'Here, the wild Normalis forages in its natural habitat. It does not know it is being studied. It does not know anything.' Captions are generated from real observations of native behaviour. *Why:* Makes the dull but essential scouting step entertaining while quietly stating the measurable facts the player needs to copy. *Risk:* Scouting captions drawn from live data need careful templating or they will repeat within one dish.
102. **Rivals Who Suspect You: Neighbour Natives Gossip** (medium, full). A thin ticker beneath the dish shows native 'chatter' as chemical signals rendered as comic text bubbles: 'Has anyone else noticed the new guy never takes a day off?' The bubbles rise with suspicion channel and become an early warning that is funnier than a bar. *Why:* Gives suspicion a face and a voice without any illustration, and doubles as a readable, charming alarm layer before the formal immune stages. *Risk:* Players may read bubbles as flavour and ignore them, so each must map to exactly one channel to stay useful.
103. **Hexagon Seams Get Embarrassed** (medium, full). When suspicion rises and hex seams show through the robot cells, each exposed cell briefly flashes a blush-pink stain and tries to wobble back to round, with a tiny synth 'oh no' two-note slide. Cells actively hide the seams until they cannot. *Why:* Turns the exposure visual from a warning into a character beat, which helps players read the escalating tell at a glance. *Risk:* Per-cell animation must be driven by a shader uniform, not individual state, to stay cheap.

Choices give no mechanical bonus except a flavour line in the report, but a few choices secretly match what natives actually do and grant a tiny mimicry hint. *Why:* A cheap, funny, low-pressure choice that rewards observation and breaks up the loop with a character moment. *Risk:* Players will feel cheated if the secret match is never explained, so the autopsy must credit it when it matters.

After a failed run, a short procedural 'highlights reel' strip shows three frames from the replay chosen by peak suspicion events, each captioned in deadpan: 'Frame 1: We thought no one would notice the hexagons.' Each stamp makes a satisfying synth thunk. *Why:* Rewards the inverted goal of being unremarkable with a joke that reinforces it, and gives the win state a distinct, shareable look. *Risk:* Stamps can feel hollow if wording never changes, so they need at least eight variants tied to specific margins.
